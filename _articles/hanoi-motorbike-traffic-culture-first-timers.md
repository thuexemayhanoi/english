New arrivals watch a Hanoi junction — a river of motorbikes flowing around a corner, through each other, somehow without contact — and conclude it is chaos. It is not. Hanoi traffic is one of the most rule-governed systems you will ever ride in; the rules simply are not written on signs. This is the field guide to the unwritten code, because understanding it is what makes your first week safe instead of terrifying.

## The first principle: negotiation, not obedience

Vietnamese traffic law exists and is enforced, but the daily system runs on a continuous, real-time negotiation. Right of way is not taken by lane position alone; it is granted by the other party based on your speed, trajectory and eye contact. The deep habit is **yield to what is bigger and assert what is smaller**: pedestrians yield to bikes, bikes to cars and trucks, everyone to the bus — and the bus driver, in return, gives the bikes room. When you ride, you are constantly signalling your intent with your behaviour: a steady line says "I am continuing"; a glance and a small drift says "I am coming through here."

## The horn is a language

Hanoi is loud because the horn here is not aggression — it is communication. A short beep means "I am here," spoken into your blind spot; two beeps at a junction mean "I am going"; a long press is the rare genuine complaint. Nobody is angry at you. Learn the vocabulary and use it: announcing yourself into someone's blind spot is good manners here, and silence — gliding up unheard beside a bus — is the genuinely dangerous act. The etiquette guide covers the finer points.

## How the junctions actually work

The formal rules are red, amber, green. The working rules: **flow, don't fight.** Traffic streams merge like braided rivers, each rider holding a gap and moving through it. The technique — covered in the filtering guide — is to keep moving at a steady, predictable pace, never fully stopping in the middle of a live junction, and never accelerating unpredictably. Right on red is generally not permitted, but expect locals to roll it — and expect the police campaign of the month to change the enforcement temperature. Follow the local flow and stay humble.

## The sidewalk question

Riders use sidewalks — as parking, as loading zones, sometimes as overflow lanes. This is illegal in its riding form and the police do run sidewalk-riding campaigns, but you will see it hourly. As a visitor, do not copy it: you stand out, enforcement is unpredictable, and pedestrians have the right of it. Park on sidewalks where the bikes park; do not ride them.

## How to fit in during week one

Riding like a local means: predictable speed, no sudden lane changes, lights on, horn used gently, mirrors checked constantly, and — above all — **calm**. Aggression reads as unpredictability, and unpredictability is the one thing this system cannot absorb. Start on the wide districts, graduate to the Old Quarter, and give yourself the first day after rush hour, not inside it.

And a final cultural note: Hanoi riders are generous to struggling foreigners. A wobble and a nervous smile at a red light will produce offers of help, directions and sometimes an escort. The system has absorbed millions of learners; it will absorb you too.

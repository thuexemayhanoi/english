Running out of fuel in Hanoi takes effort — the city has one of the densest petrol-station networks anywhere, with a forecourt never more than a few minutes away. But using them is not entirely like fueling up at home, and a few local conventions are worth knowing before your first fill.

## How stations work

Most urban stations are attendant-served for motorbikes: you ride to the bike lane, tell the attendant the amount ("lam ngan" — a hundred thousand dong — or "day" — fill it up) or just the fuel grade, and they do the rest. Payment is cash or QR-code transfer at the booth; staff make change without ceremony. Tipping is not expected. At busy stations, attendants coordinate the bike lanes like air-traffic control — follow their pointing, and do not worry about perfection; they have seen worse riders than you today already.

Some stations have introduced self-service pump areas for motorbikes in recent years; the convention there is the universal one — helmet off, engine off, phone away, nozzle in tank.

## What fuel to buy

The fuel-grade question has its own full guide for Vietnam: the common retail grades are RON 95 in its variants (including the E5 ethanol-blend RON95), with higher-octane premium options at many city stations. For the underbones and scooters that make up nearly all rental fleets, standard RON 95 is the safe default; premium grades cost more and matter little unless a manufacturer specifies them. When renting, ask the shop what the bike takes — most will say "95" — and keep it consistent.

## Density and the low-fuel game

Station coverage in the central wards is good but not infinite, and the useful habit is the local one: fill at half, not at fumes. Fuel gauges on small bikes are famously approximate (their own guide explains why), and the stations that are easiest to enter on a bike are rarely the ones closest to where your tank finally gives up. Two-wheelers queue separately from cars at most stations; the bike lanes move fast.

## Practical notes

- **Rain**: canopies cover the pumps; the station forecourt is often the driest fifty metres in the ward during a storm — a legitimate place to wait it out.
- **Night**: city stations stay open late, some around the clock; the nocturnal markets and airport runs never leave you stranded.
- **Cash**: small notes are welcome; the largest note anyone wants at a bike pump is a hundred thousand.
- **Fire rules**: standard everywhere — engine off, no smoking, no phone calls at the pump.
- **Jerry cans**: carrying spare fuel in cans on a motorbike is restricted and generally unnecessary given station density; rental customers should simply fill up rather than improvising fuel logistics.

## When something goes wrong

If a rental bike sputters after a fill (a misfueling suspicion, or bad fuel from a rural stop), do not keep riding it hard — call the rental shop for guidance. The fuel-system guides cover the symptoms and the fixes; the practical Hanoi answer is that a mechanic who will drain a tank is never more than a short push away.

Filling up is the most boring five minutes of a Hanoi rider's week — which is exactly the standard a fuel network should meet.

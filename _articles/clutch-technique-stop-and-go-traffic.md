Hanoi traffic is ten thousand small launches. Riders who master clutch finesse in stop-and-go conditions stop thinking about the gearbox within a week; riders who fight it stay tired and frustrated.

## Creeping is the core skill

In nose-to-tail flow you rarely need a full launch. Hold the bike in first, ride the friction zone like a dimmer switch — a little more to advance, a little less to hold — and cover the rear brake so you can stop instantly. The throttle stays almost constant. Speed is controlled at the lever.

## Spacing beats speed

Leave a bike-length cushion ahead and watch two or three vehicles ahead, not the wheel in front. The gaps in Vietnamese traffic are dynamic; a smooth rider who moves with the wave arrives almost as fast as a rider who sprints every gap, with far less clutch work and far less risk.

## Junctions and bridges

Approach junctions with the clutch covered and a gear already chosen — usually second if rolling, first if slow. Our left-turn guide covers crossing traffic, and the Hanoi bridges guide covers the merge-and-flow rhythm that bridge traffic demands.

## Keeping the clutch healthy

Feathering is normal use, but hours of it heats the clutch plates. Do not hold the bike on the friction zone on a slope when the rear brake could hold it instead, and take neutral at long stops rather than holding the lever for minutes. If the engine revs rise without the bike accelerating, the clutch is slipping — our clutch troubleshooting article covers what that means and what to do about it on a rental.

## One honest note for renters

Heavy stop-and-go is where a manual bike is most work and an automatic scooter least. If your stay is short and your route is dense city traffic, a semi-automatic or scooter may serve you better; our semi-automatic renting guide covers that option.

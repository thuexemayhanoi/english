The Temple of Literature — Van Mieu–Quoc Tu Giam — is the oldest university site in Vietnam: founded in 1070 under Emperor Ly Thanh Tong, dedicated to Confucius, and soon after the home of the country's first national academy. For a thousand years it has been where the culture honours learning itself, and it remains the calmest landmark in central Hanoi — a walled garden of courtyards that shuts out the traffic entirely.

## What you are visiting

The complex runs through five courtyards from the Great Gate inward: the central way through the Kiem Pond to the Constellation of Literature pavilion — the site's icon — then the stele gardens, where the names of royal examination laureates were carved onto stone tablets carried on the backs of turtles. The deeper courtyards hold the academy buildings and the Confucian sanctuary. Give it ninety unhurried minutes; the stele house and the pavilion are the photographs, but the back courtyards are the quiet.

## The route

Van Mieu sits on Van Mieu street at the edge of the old Dong Da–Ba Dinh border, a few minutes' ride southwest of the Old Quarter. The approach is wide-street riding — Nguyen Khuyen and Van Mieu streets serve the entrance — and the district around it is university territory, busy with students on bikes. Ride the approach at student pace, park, and walk in.

## Parking

There is dedicated motorbike parking around the temple complex, with attendants — among the more reliable landmark lots in central Hanoi. The side streets fill during graduation-photo season and exam-prayer periods, when families bring students to pray for results; on those days the lots are the only realistic option, and the surrounding streets are double-parked for a block in every direction.

## Making the visit work

The temple anchors the central heritage ride: pair it with the Imperial Citadel to its north — the natural order is Van Mieu in the morning, the citadel before lunch — and add the Fine Arts Museum around the corner if the day has room. The area guide for the district covers the wider streets. Early morning is the best time in every sense: cooler, quieter, and the courtyards' incense-and-old-stone atmosphere is not yet shared with a hundred tour groups.

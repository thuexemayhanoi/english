Most "aggressive" behaviour on Vietnamese roads is not anger — it is a driving culture where space is claimed rather than granted, and it feels hostile only until you read it as traffic language. The real danger is the genuine aggressive driver: the one who weaves, honks at the world, and treats every gap as a challenge. How you respond decides whether you arrive or become part of their accident.

## Never compete for a win

On a motorbike you lose every physical argument by default. Do not brake-check, race, or ride beside a driver who has already shown you they do not check mirrors. Fall back, let them go, and let the distance grow — the cost is seconds, and the alternative is riding inside someone else's mistake.

## De-escalate with position

If a driver is tailgating, move aside rather than speeding up. If someone cuts you off, treat it as information about them, not an insult to you: slow, create space, continue. Steady, boring predictability is your protection — defensive-riding-motorbike-vietnam covers the habits that keep you out of these situations in the first place.

## Do not gesture back

A return gesture only converts "bad driver" into "conflict." If you were wrong, a small apologetic nod ends it — Vietnamese drivers read it the same as anywhere. Never dismount to argue; stay on the bike, which is your exit.

## When it turns into a chase

If someone follows you after an incident, do not lead them home. Stop at a busy public place — a petrol station is ideal: lit, busy, with staff and cameras. Most followers disappear the moment there are witnesses. ## Your attention is your armour

Anger narrows attention, and aggressive drivers punish narrow attention. Riding angry after an incident is as dangerous as riding drunk — give yourself a kilometre to let it fade, and keep your eyes on the mirrors and blind spots that matter — riding-motorbike-blind-spots-vietnam.

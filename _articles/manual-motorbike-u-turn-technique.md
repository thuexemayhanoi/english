A tight U-turn on a manual bike is a slow-speed clutch exercise with a corner in it. The ingredients never change: eyes up, steady throttle, clutch in the friction zone, rear brake as your speed regulator, and a wide bar turn. Get those five right and the bike will turn tighter than you expect.

## Set it up before you turn

Check behind, signal, and start the turn from the edge of your lane so you have the full width. Look over your shoulder at where you want to finish — the bike follows your head, not the front wheel. First gear, engine at a fast idle, clutch just in the friction zone.

## Through the turn

Keep three things constant and let one thing change:

- **Constant throttle** — set before the turn and leave it alone.
- **Constant clutch position** — a hair of slip, feeding just enough power to roll.
- **Constant rear-brake pressure** — this is your speed dial. More brake tightens the arc; less opens it.
- **Turning bars** — turn progressively, more than feels natural, because at walking pace the front wheel does the steering.

The head stays up and turned to the exit point the whole way. The classic failure is looking at the kerb you are afraid of hitting — the bike goes straight to it.

## The size of the arc

With good technique a small underbone turns inside two parking spaces. Where the road is genuinely too narrow — a one-way lane with parked bikes both sides — do the two-step turn: stop, walk the bike around using the bars and your legs, remount. This is not failure; delivery riders in the Old Quarter do it a hundred times a day.

## Uphill and downhill U-turns

Uphill needs more revs and a slower clutch release, and a little more commitment — hesitating mid-turn on a slope is where bikes fall over. Downhill needs more rear brake and almost no clutch, letting the engine's idle pull the bike around. Practise both on a quiet slope before trying either in traffic.

## Practise with paint, not cars

Find an empty section of parking and mark a lane-width strip on the ground. Turn within it, both directions, until it is boring. Then narrow it. Two sessions of this and the real-world version — the U-turn at the end of a bridge ramp or the mid-lane turn when your route betrays you — becomes automatic.

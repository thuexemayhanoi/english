The hardest slow-speed moments on a manual motorbike are not about speed at all — they are about staying calm at one or two kilometres per hour while your feet stay on the pegs. Queuing at a Hanoi junction, edging through a market lane, or shuffling forward in a river of bikes all demand walking-pace control, and that control comes from the clutch and rear brake working against each other.

## The core skill: clutch against rear brake

Creeping is a controlled tug-of-war. Pull the clutch in just past the friction zone so the engine wants to move the bike, and hold the rear brake lightly to stop it running away. The throttle stays steady — a small, constant opening, enough to keep the engine above idle. Then you steer with the bars and balance with your knees and hips. Feather the clutch to go, feather the rear brake to hold back. Front brake stays out of this game entirely: pulling it while the bars are turned at walking pace is the classic way to drop the bike.

## Where to put your eyes

Look ahead, not down. At walking pace the bike goes where your eyes go, and staring at the front tyre makes the wobbles worse. Keep your head up, look at where you want the bike to be in three seconds, and let the handlebars make small corrections rather than big ones.

## Progressive drills

1. **Straight-line creep.** On a quiet stretch, ride the friction zone at the slowest speed you can manage without putting a foot down. Repeat until the engine note and clutch feel predictable.
2. **Brake-held creep.** Same exercise, but now hold the rear brake constantly and slip the clutch against it. The bike should crawl in tiny increments. This is the exact skill you need in junction queues.
3. **The box.** Mark four objects on the ground about four metres apart and creep a square around them, including two tight ninety-degree turns.
4. **The figure of eight.** The graduation exercise: two slow circles crossing in the middle. If you can do this without feet down, junction creep will feel easy.

## Feet and posture

Heels on the pegs, balls of the feet ready, knees gripped against the tank. Dragging both feet along the ground "just in case" is a habit worth breaking early — it removes your balance input and teaches the bike to wobble. Practise instead hovering the feet a centimetre above the road, and only put a foot down when you actually stop.

## What goes wrong, and the fix

- **Stalling**: you let the clutch out too far while the brake holds the bike. Fix: more revs, less clutch, more patience.
- **Lurching**: throttle spikes. Fix: hold a steady small opening — the clutch is your accelerator, not the twist grip.
- **Wobbling into a stop**: you looked down or grabbed the front brake. Fix: eyes up, rear brake only.

Slow-speed clutch work is also the safest possible practice: it barely uses fuel, overheats nothing, and the consequences of a mistake are a foot down.

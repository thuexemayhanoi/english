A monthly rental can be a sensible way to build riding skill across weeks instead of cramming it into a holiday. But learning to ride on Hanoi streets carries conditions that matter before you start.

The short answer: a month gives you time, not permission. A bike in the licence-free moped class can be ridden from age 16, a bike above that class needs a licence valid in Vietnam for its class, and either way the first sessions belong away from traffic. This guide sets out the licence position, a four-week practice plan, a way to log progress, the signs that mean it is time for lessons, and the questions to put to the shop and to your insurer.

## Licence reality while you learn

Above the moped class, in Vietnam you need the correct licence to ride on public roads, even while learning. Riding without a licence carries fines and possible vehicle impoundment — the specifics are in [The Fine for Riding Without a Licence in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/no-licence-fine-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) and [Renting a Motorbike in Hanoi Without a Licence: The Real Risks]({{ '/articles/renting-a-motorbike-hanoi-without-licence/' | relative_url }}). There is no legal "learner" exception for the streets. Practise legally: off-road or quiet private areas for the first hours, and only street riding once you hold the right licence for the bike, or when the bike is in the licence-free moped class.

A quiet space is a safety choice rather than a legal exemption. This guide cannot say whether a given yard or car park counts as a road, so ask whoever controls the space before you use it, and plan as though the licence rules follow you onto it.

### Two starting points: the moped class and everything above it

Law 36/2024/QH15 puts a new rider in one of two positions. Article 34(1)(g) defines a moped as a two- or three-wheeled motor vehicle with a design speed of no more than 50 km/h and either a combustion engine of 50 cm3 or less or an electric motor of 4 kW or less; a motorised pedal bicycle is not a moped. Article 59(1)(a) lets a person aged 16 or over ride one. [Renting a Motorbike in Hanoi as a Complete Beginner]({{ '/articles/renting-motorbike-hanoi-complete-beginner/' | relative_url }}) sets out what the law asks of a moped rider in place of a licence, and it is the page to read before you treat a small-engine bike as a way round the licence.

Above that class the question is the usual one. Article 57(1)(a) gives class A1 to two-wheeled motorbikes with a cylinder capacity of up to 125 cm3 or an electric motor of up to 11 kW, class A covers what is larger (Article 57(1)(b)), and Article 59(1)(b) sets 18 as the age for being issued class A1. [What an A1 licence covers]({{ '/articles/a1-licence-vietnam-what-you-can-ride/' | relative_url }}) has the detail by engine size.

| Your starting point | What the law says | What it means for the month |
|---|---|---|
| Aged 16 or over, on a bike that meets the moped definition | Articles 34(1)(g) and 59(1)(a): a moped, from age 16 | No licence class applies, so the plan below is about skill; ask for the engine size or motor power and the design speed of the exact bike |
| Aged 18 or over, on a petrol bike up to 125 cm3 or an electric bike up to 11 kW | Article 57(1)(a): class A1 | A licence valid in Vietnam for that class is needed before street riding |
| On a bike larger than that | Article 57(1)(b): class A | A licence valid in Vietnam for class A is needed |

The Law's one learner provision that this guide read, Article 56(5), concerns people learning to drive cars. It creates no stage for a motorbike rider, and a rental agreement does not create one either.

### If you stay longer than a visit

[The Long-Term Rider's Licence Checklist for Hanoi]({{ '/articles/long-term-rider-licence-checklist-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) covers matching the class to the bike, the documents a foreign rider carries and tracking expiry dates, so this guide does not repeat it.

One rule matters if you are a foreign resident who ends up holding a Vietnamese licence. Article 24(5)(a) of Circular 12/2025/TT-BCA says the term of a Vietnamese licence issued in exchange to a foreigner follows the term shown on the residence document and on the foreign licence, and does not exceed the term the Vietnamese licence itself allows. A newer Ministry of Public Security circular on the same subject, Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, took effect on 1 July 2026. This guide has not read its text on foreigners, so treat the earlier rule as a question to ask, not as the current answer.

Whether a foreign resident may sit the Vietnamese test, or exchange a foreign licence, and which documents the office wants, is a question for the licensing authority. This guide describes no steps and no fees.

## Choosing a learning bike

If you are renting for a month to build skill, pick a light, forgiving machine — [Easiest Motorbikes for Beginner Riders in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/easiest-motorbikes-for-beginners-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) compares what new riders actually manage well. An automatic removes one whole skill (gears and clutch) from the learning load; [Automatic or Semi-Automatic for a Monthly Rental in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/automatic-or-semi-automatic-monthly-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) covers that trade-off for long rentals.

### What changes when the rental lasts a month

On a day rental a wrong bike is an irritation; on a month it is a wrong bike for weeks. Tell the shop you are learning when you enquire, and ask which light automatic is free on your dates; contact us to confirm current availability of any model, because no bike is guaranteed.

Sit on the bike with the engine off, wearing the shoes you will ride in, and check that both feet reach the ground and your hands reach every control without stretching. Ask now whether a swap after the first week is possible and on what terms; no swap policy is published here. A common mistake is moving to a bigger bike because confidence has arrived: a bigger engine adds speed before it adds skill, and Article 57(1)(a) draws the line at 125 cm3 or 11 kW.

## A month-long learning path

Week one: controls, slow-speed balance and braking in empty spaces, early morning. Week two: quiet residential streets at low speed. Week three: busier roads at off-peak times, basic traffic reading. Week four: normal routes at normal times. The one skill you cannot skip is emergency braking practice — in a safe place, not in traffic.

Read these weeks as a suggested plan, not as a measure of how long learning takes. This guide has no figure for how many weeks a new rider needs, and a rider who needs a second week at any stage stays in it.

### Week one: controls and balance in an empty space

Start with the engine off and find every control by touch: both brake levers, throttle, indicators, horn, starter and stand. Then, in an empty space with room to run on after a mistake and a smooth, dry surface, pull away without a lurch, stop on a chosen mark with both brakes, hold a straight line at walking pace and ride wide turns before tight ones.

Practise the emergency stop here, from a modest speed in a straight line, using the method in [Emergency Braking on a Motorbike in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/emergency-braking-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}). The readiness test in the complete-beginner guide is a ready-made checklist for the end of the week. Keep sessions short, and dress for the fall you hope not to have: a fastened helmet, closed shoes and long trousers.

### Week two: quiet streets at quiet hours

Judge quiet on the street itself, at the hour you plan to ride, instead of assuming a day or time is empty. Choose one short loop with a clear way out and ride it repeatedly; a familiar route frees attention for the traffic. Add early signalling, a shoulder check while holding your line, smooth stops and starts at junctions, and room when passing parked bikes. A rushed session is a signal to go back to week one's exercises for a day.

### Week three: busier roads away from the peak

Pick roads busier than the loop but not at the hour they are busiest, again judged by looking. The new skill is reading traffic: look well ahead, hold a predictable line, leave space and use your mirrors before you slow or turn. Talk yourself through each junction as you approach it, asking who could cross and who could pull out, and do not copy the gaps other riders take, because they have a baseline you are still building. Repeat the emergency stop once a week in your quiet space.

### Week four and after: normal routes, then rain, darkness and a passenger

Take a normal route at a normal time in dry daylight first. Only then add harder conditions, one at a time.

- **Rain.** Wet roads lengthen stopping distances, and painted lines and metal covers can be slippery. Make the first wet ride a short, familiar one, and read [Riding a Rental Motorbike in Hanoi Rain: What to Know]({{ '/articles/riding-rental-motorbike-hanoi-rain/' | relative_url }}) first.
- **Darkness.** Check at handover that the headlight, tail light, brake light and indicators all work, and read [Riding a Motorbike at Night in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/night-riding-motorbike-safety-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) before the first night ride.
- **A passenger, last.** A passenger changes balance and braking. Carry one only where the law and the rental terms allow it, as [Carrying Passengers on a Motorbike in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/motorbike-passenger-rules-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) explains, and repeat week one's slow-speed exercises with that person on the seat before you go onto the street.

## Logging progress

A short written log turns "I feel better" into something you can check. Write it straight after each session, in a notes app or a small notebook.

| Record | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Date, time and place, with the light and the road surface | Shows whether you are adding difficulty one step at a time |
| The exercise or route, marked dull, steady or hard | A hard that repeats is the signal to stay at this stage |
| Any near miss, fall or moment of freezing, in a sentence | Patterns across weeks become the case for lessons |
| What the bike did: brake feel, noises, pulling to one side, tyres | Gives you words for the shop and a baseline for normal |

Move to the next stage when the current one reads dull across several sessions in a row, and not before.

## When to stop and get lessons

Stopping is part of the plan, not a failure of it. These signs in the log or on the road mean more solo practice is not working:

- You still grab the front brake, or cannot stop on a chosen mark, after repeated sessions in the empty space.
- You freeze or look down at the controls at junctions.
- Low-speed drops, or near drops, keep happening.
- Fear grows instead of fading, or you start avoiding the bike.

Lessons here means time with a riding instructor beside you in a space that is not traffic. This guide names no school and gives no fee: ask people who have learned in Hanoi for recommendations, and check what a lesson covers and what it costs before you book.

While you pause, the rental keeps running. Early return carries no automatic refund for unused time, so [Ending a Monthly Motorbike Rental Early]({{ '/articles/early-return-monthly-rental-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) is worth reading before you commit, and it is one reason a rider who has never ridden is better served by a short rental first than by a month.

## Insurance while you learn

Nguyen Tu does not provide motorbike insurance, so any cover is yours to arrange. A learner is exactly the rider a policy's small print may catch, so ask your insurer before the first session:

- Does the policy cover riding a motorbike at all, and does it set an engine-size limit?
- Does it require a licence valid for the bike's class, and what happens to a claim if you ride a moped without one?
- Does the cover last the whole month, or is it written for a shorter trip?
- Does it say anything about riders who are still learning?

[Arranging Your Own Insurance as a Long-Term Renter in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/insurance-for-long-term-renter-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) covers the wider picture for a long rental.

## What to ask the shop

Beyond telling the shop you are learning and asking about a light bike and a swap, put these to it by message or call. They are questions, not rules it is known to follow:

- What is the monthly rate for that bike, and what deposit does it need? The deposit is typically 2,000,000–5,000,000 VND depending on the vehicle and conditions.
- What maintenance may be included over the month, and which consumables, such as inner tubes or tyres, would be mine? Maintenance may include an oil change, chain adjustment and tension, tyre-pressure checks and normal inspection, while consumables may be the renter's responsibility.
- If I decide after two weeks that I am not ready, what happens to the rest of the rental?

## Why a month helps

Short rentals push learners onto busy roads too fast. A month lets skill develop before you filter between buses in rush hour. Combine it with the weekly check routine in [A Month with a Rental Motorbike: Inspection and Check Routine]({{ '/articles/monthly-rental-inspection-schedule-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) so you also learn how a healthy bike should feel — new riders who know "normal" notice problems earlier.

Because the bike stays the same for weeks, small changes stand out, and the log above is your baseline for reporting them to the shop.

## Common questions

### Can I learn to ride on a monthly rental in Hanoi?

Above the moped class, only with a licence valid in Vietnam for that bike's class; a bike in the licence-free moped class can be ridden from age 16. In both cases the first practice belongs away from traffic.

### How long does it take to learn?

This guide gives no figure, because the plan runs on stages, not days. Move to the next week when the current one feels dull in the log.

### Do I need lessons?

The signs in the lessons section above are the cue: a rider who freezes at junctions, keeps dropping the bike at low speed or grows more afraid is better served by an instructor than by more solo hours.

### Does my travel insurance cover me while I learn?

Do not assume it does. Nguyen Tu does not provide motorbike insurance, and a policy may exclude motorbikes or set licence conditions, so read the exclusions before the first session.

## The one-line version

Learn legally — licence first, quiet places first — choose a light automatic or semi-automatic, and let the month build your skill gradually instead of all at once. Above the moped class, "licence first" means a licence valid in Vietnam for the bike's class; on a licence-free moped it means the age of 16 and real control of the bike.

The direct answer: an IDP counts in Vietnam for no more than three years from its issue date, and only while the national licence behind it is still valid. Article 57 of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (No. 36/2024/QH15) defines the international driving permit it recognises that way, so the earlier of the two dates sets the limit.

The dates sit in three places in the Law as consolidated in March 2026 (text 55/VBHN-VPQH): clause 6(b) lets a permit and a national licence count at all, clause 8(a) fixes the permit's term and clause 7 lists when a licence stops having effect. Whether your country's permit counts in the first place is answered in [which countries' permits count]({{ '/articles/which-countries-idp-valid-vietnam/' | relative_url }}).

## How the Law sets the term

Clause 8(a) of Article 57 describes the permit that Vietnam recognises. In our translation of the Vietnamese text, it is issued on a uniform form by the competent state authority of a Vienna Convention member state, runs for no more than three years counted from the day it was issued, and has to fit within whatever validity the national licence still has. "No more than" makes three years a ceiling, not a promise: a permit issued for a shorter term has the shorter term.

### Two clocks, one finish line

The permit runs on two clocks at once. One starts on its issue date and stops after three years at the latest. The other is the national licence's own term, which the permit cannot outlast. Clause 8(b) asks a rider to carry both documents, each matched to the class of bike being ridden and not past its term, so the pair works only until the first of the two clocks stops.

Clause 8 adds no third clock. It does not tie the permit to the length of a visa or to the days since arrival; the one place it mentions a stay is 8(c), which limits a fixed-period suspension of the holder's right to use the licence to the time the holder may lawfully reside in Vietnam. Article 30(2) of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, however, hands a permit recognised under a treaty to that treaty, a point the resident section below returns to.

### When a licence has no effect

Clause 7 names two cases in which a licence has no effect: its term has run out (point a) or it has been revoked under Article 62(5) (point b). Point (a) is the date rule that matters to a visitor; the texts read do not say how revocation applies to a foreign licence, so this page leaves it aside. A Vietnamese A1, A or B1 licence has no end date (Article 57(5)(a)); that rule covers Vietnamese licences, and the texts read do not say how long a foreign one lasts, so use the date printed on it.

## What actually expires

Three dates matter on the IDP rider's documents. The IDP card carries an issue and expiry date set by the issuing authority in the convention's home country — typically one to three years. The Law's three-year ceiling and licence tie apply on top of that, and a booklet may print no end date at all, so read the dates on your own booklet rather than relying on a typical range. The national licence has its own validity, which the IDP cannot outlive: [a permit translating an expired licence]({{ '/articles/idp-vietnam-motorbike-rules/' | relative_url }}) translates nothing. And the rider's residence status, while not printed on the permit or the licence, changes the practical answer — a qualifying visa or residence card (Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, Article 24(1)(a)) opens the [exchange route]({{ '/articles/foreign-licence-exchange-vietnam/' | relative_url }}), and [long-termers]({{ '/articles/expat-motorbike-licence-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) are expected onto it.

### What the booklet shows

The only permit form read for this page is the one Vietnam prints for its own permits (Appendix XI to Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, set by Article 27). Its cover has lines for the place of issue and a date; the appendix text shows no line for an end date. For a foreign booklet, treat the issue date as the anchor, because clause 8(a) counts from it. If your booklet also states an end date, the earlier of that date and three years after issue is the permit's limit; if it states none, work out the three-year date yourself and keep it with your documents.

| Permit issued 10 March 2026 | 10 March 2029 | 20 June 2031 | 10 March 2029, permit ceiling first |

| Permit issued 1 June 2025 | 1 June 2028 | 1 October 2026 | 1 October 2026, when the licence lapsed |

Three points follow. First, the table shows anniversaries for readability, but Article 57(8)(a) counts three years from the issue date and the texts read do not say whether the last good day is the anniversary or the day before it; the day before is a cautious choice, not a rule. Second, a licence with no end date printed leaves only one date to read, and the texts read do not say what an official does with it, so ask the licensing authority in writing.

Third, the visa is not the limit. Imagine a rider whose permit reaches its three-year ceiling in December, whose visa runs to the following March and who plans to ride until mid-January. Riding on the pair has to stop in December, or the rider has to move to another route such as exchange, if they qualify.

## The tourist case

For a visitor inside the permit's validity, the rule is stable: carry both documents, ride the categories the national licence actually includes, and the arrangement lasts as long as the trip. There is no "tourist grace period" to worry about because the permit itself is the arrangement.

Article 57 gives visitors no separate allowance of days. A visitor needs three numbers: the permit's issue date plus three years, the licence's end date and the last day of riding they plan, with the first two both falling after the third. [Can tourists legally ride a motorbike in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/can-tourists-ride-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) covers the wider question, and [what a rider must carry]({{ '/articles/documents-to-carry-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) covers the remaining papers.

## The resident case

The exchange conditions of Circular 12/2025/TT-BCA — three months of residence papers and a valid national licence — mark where the IDP stops being the right answer. Those were the conditions until 30 June 2026. Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA replaced that circular on 1 July 2026 (Article 41(6)): exchange now needs a national licence still in term plus one of the papers in Article 24(1)(a), such as a visa or temporary residence card with at least 90 days remaining or a valid permanent residence card, and qualifying to exchange is not the same thing as the IDP ceasing to work.

Nothing in clause 7 or 8 of Article 57 invalidates the permit on the day a rider first qualifies to exchange; what changes is that exchange is open, and Article 18(7) of the Circular requires a foreigner who needs to drive in Vietnam and has a national licence to exchange it for the matching Vietnamese class (point a), unless a licensing treaty that Vietnam has joined provides differently (point b).

In our reading the 1968 pair of Article 57(6)(b) is that treaty exception, which is why a visitor can ride on it. Neither point of Article 18(7) sets a date by which a resident on the pair must switch, so anyone staying beyond a short trip should ask the traffic police for a written answer on which route applies and keep it with their documents.

### The notice printed on Vietnam's own booklet

One more printed text bears on a long stay. The inside front cover of the permit that Vietnam issues (Appendix XI, in Vietnamese and English) says the permit "is not valid for the territory of Socialist Republic of Vietnam", and also: "This permit shall cease to be valid in the territory of another Contracting Party if its holder establishes his normal residence there."

That is the wording on the form Vietnam prints, not a clause of the Law, and the Convention's own articles were not read for this page. It is a reason to ask, not a rule this page can state: nothing in clauses 7 and 8 of Article 57 says a permit lapses when its holder takes up residence, the texts read do not define "normal residence" or say how an official applies the notice to a foreign booklet, and Article 30(2) sends a treaty-recognised permit to the treaty. A rider who has moved to Vietnam should check whether their own booklet carries similar wording, then ask in writing.

### What exchange does to the dates

Exchange replaces the pair with one Vietnamese licence, and the dates change shape. Article 24(5)(a) sets the end date of the exchanged licence from two sources, one of the qualifying papers (the visa or residence card behind the application) and the foreign licence's own term, capped at the term the Vietnamese class carries.

Article 24(1)(c) adds that an expired foreign licence is not exchanged, and neither is an IDP. The national licence's expiry date therefore closes both routes: a rider who lets the home licence lapse has no working pair and nothing to exchange. [The foreign licence exchange guide]({{ '/articles/foreign-licence-exchange-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) has the dossier and the filing routes, and [the long-term rider's licence checklist for Hanoi]({{ '/articles/long-term-rider-licence-checklist-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) puts every date on one list.

### A permit issued by Vietnam starts no clock

Article 30(1) gives a permit that Vietnam itself issues under the 1968 Convention no validity for use inside Vietnam. No date printed on such a permit extends riding here, so it is no answer to a lapsing pair. [The 1968 Vienna Convention guide]({{ '/articles/1968-vienna-convention-idp-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) covers these permits and the United Nations record.

## What riders get wrong

The common failure is the half-pair: the IDP carried while the national licence sits at the hotel, which leaves the rider without a valid licence at any stop, because the permit counts only together with the national licence. The second is the category mismatch: an IDP cannot extend a car-only licence to a scooter. The expiry dates are the easy part — the document pairing is what checkpoints check.

Dates cause their own mistakes:

- **Counting from arrival.** The Law counts the three years from the issue date, not from the day you land, so a permit issued two years before your trip has at most one year left.
- **Assuming a renewed licence carries the old permit.** Clause 8(a) still asks the permit to fit the licence's remaining term, and the texts read do not say how an older permit is treated against a new licence document. Ask the permit's issuing authority before relying on the old booklet.

What a stop with a lapsed pair costs is set in a penalty decree, not in Article 57, so this page quotes no amount. [The no-licence fine guide]({{ '/articles/no-licence-fine-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) gives figures under the 2024 decree text, which was amended in 2026, so check any figure against the amended decree.

## Checking your dates before you ride

1. Find the issue date on the permit and add three years. That is the ceiling.
2. If the booklet also states an end date, note it; the earlier of the two is the permit's limit.
3. Read the national licence's end date. If it shows none, ask its issuer, as the worked examples suggest.
4. Take the earliest of the dates from steps 1 to 3. That is your last riding day on the pair.
5. Set it against the last day you plan to ride. If it falls short, choose between ending riding earlier, asking the competent authority of a Vienna Convention state about a new permit, or exchange if you qualify.

## Quick answers

### Is an IDP valid for three years in Vietnam?

At most. Article 57(8)(a) makes three years from the issue date the longest term and requires the permit to fit the national licence's remaining validity, so the earlier date wins.

### Does an IDP stay valid if my home licence expires?

No. A licence whose term has ended has no effect (Article 57(7)(a)), and clause 8(b) asks you to carry both documents in term. Neither an expired foreign licence nor an IDP can be exchanged for a Vietnamese licence (Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, Article 24(1)(c)).

### Is there a maximum number of days I can ride on an IDP?

Not in Article 57, which sets no day count or maximum stay for the pair; its limits are the permit's three-year ceiling and the licence's term. For a rider who has settled in Vietnam, the printed notice and Article 18(7) raise a separate question that the resident section above discusses, so ask the traffic police in writing.

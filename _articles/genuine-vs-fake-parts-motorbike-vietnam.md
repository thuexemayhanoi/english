Vietnam's parts market is enormous and mostly honest — and it includes counterfeits of exactly the parts whose failure matters most: brake pads, chains, plugs, filters and bearings. Knowing how the market works is part of owning a bike here.

## What gets faked

The pattern is universal: high-volume consumables for popular models. Brake pads that look identical to genuine ones but with a friction material that fades; chains with a brand's logo and a fraction of its strength; spark plugs and oil filters copied because they are easy to sell. Fairings and mirrors copied cosmetically are harmless by comparison — the danger list is anything that stops, holds or filters.

## How to get genuine parts

Say the word: "phụ tùng chính hãng" (genuine parts) is a normal request, not a fussy one. Brand dealers and authorised workshops for Honda and Yamaha carry genuine parts by default; independent workshops can order them on request. Packaging is a check — genuine packaging in Vietnam is usually well-printed with part numbers that match what is inside; suspiciously loose packaging with misprinted logos is a signal. For brake components especially, asking to see the part and its box before it is fitted is reasonable. Brake-pads-replacement-motorbike-vietnam covers the work itself.

## Price as a signal

Genuine parts cost more than copies — a genuine pad set for a common model is recognisably more expensive than the cheapest option on the shelf. A "genuine" price that is suspiciously cheap is a question worth asking. This article does not quote part prices: they vary by model and shop, and the shop's quote is the honest reference.

## The renter's angle

On a rental, parts are the shop's problem — your job is reporting problems and letting them repair; outside-repair-shops-rental-motorbike covers how any roadside repair interacts with the rental agreement. Do not buy parts for a rental bike yourself.

## The one-line version

The fakes copy the parts that matter most — say "chính hãng", buy safety-critical parts through brand workshops, and treat too-cheap "genuine" as a question, not a bargain.

Northern Vietnam's storms arrive fast: a blue sky becomes a black one in twenty minutes, and the first minutes of a storm are its most dangerous — wind before rain, drivers panicking, and riders speeding to beat it. The best riders know that stopping is sometimes the whole skill.

## Read the warning order

A storm usually announces itself in sequence: wind gusts swinging trees, a wall of dark cloud, temperature dropping, then a squall line of rain. When you feel the gusts, that is your window to choose a stopping place — ten minutes later, every shelter is full and every road is a river.

## Shelter like a local

Any solid cover works — a shopfront, a market overhang, a cafe, a petrol station forecourt is ideal because they are everywhere and open long hours. Avoid lone trees (lightning), billboard structures (wind) and bridge underpasses on the flooded side. Lock the bike, take your helmet inside with you, and expect the queue of bikes beside yours to grow fast — that crowd of riders is the best local advice: they all decided the same thing.

## If you must ride it out

Drop your speed hard, lights on, both brakes gentle, and expect the drivers around you to make the worst decisions of their week. riding-motorbike-heavy-rain-vietnam covers rain technique and strong-wind-riding-motorbike-vietnam the gusts — but no technique matches pulling over for twenty minutes.

## The flooding stage

The end of a big storm is when streets flood. Do not push through deep, moving water; the flooded-streets chapter of your riding day deserves its own decision-making, and flooded-streets-riding-motorbike-vietnam walks through it.

## After it passes

Check the bike once you are moving again: water finds its way in. check-motorbike-after-heavy-rain lists what to look at. And check yourself — riding shaken is riding badly; take five minutes before rejoining traffic.

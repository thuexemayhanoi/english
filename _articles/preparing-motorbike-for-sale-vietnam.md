A motorbike sells at the intersection of two stories: what it is, and how it looks. Sellers control the second completely, and it is worth real money. Preparing a bike for sale in Vietnam is a weekend job that typically repays itself in days off the sale time and hundreds of thousands of dong on the price.

## The mechanical hour of honesty

Before polishing anything, decide what gets fixed and what gets disclosed:

- **Cheap, visible, worth fixing**: brake pads, bulbs, a frayed lever, bald tyres. A buyer's checklist mirrors the used-bike inspection in used-motorbike-inspection-vietnam — anything that article tells buyers to check is what your bike will fail on.
- **Expensive or invisible**: worn top-end, tired suspension. Do not spend on big repairs for sale; price honestly and disclose. Buyers discount discovered faults twice as hard as confessed ones.
- **Nothing broken, all neglected**: then the job is fluids and adjustment — a fresh oil change, chain cleaned and adjusted, levers set, tyre pressures correct. A bike that "feels tight" in a test ride earns trust immediately.

## The deep clean

Wash and dry properly — no pressure jets at the electrics — then go further than daily washing: de-grease the engine cases, clean the chain until it is silver not black, treat any surface rust on fasteners and exhaust, dress the tyres (on the sidewall, not the tread), polish the paint. A spotless engine bay tells buyers the bike was maintained, whether or not it strictly was — and clean bikes at the same price sell first.

## The paperwork half

In Vietnam the paperwork can matter as much as the machine: the registration book, and the bike's details matching it exactly. Sort paperwork before advertising: buyers serious enough to pay well are serious about documents. If any fees or inspection obligations are outstanding, settle them — a buyer discovering them renegotiates harder than the fee costs. Verify the current transfer procedure and requirements at the time of sale; the process and its requirements are administrative matters you do not want to improvise.

## The honest service story

If you have service records, stamps or receipts, present them — they are the seller's strongest evidence, and warranty-era books add real value (the book's story is in warranty-dealer-service-vietnam). If you have no records, then: honestly describe the maintenance the bike received. Buyers have heard every claim; verifiable specifics (a named mechanic, a dated oil change) outperform adjectives — the mechanic-relationship angle is in talking-to-mechanic-vietnam.

## The listing

Photograph after the deep clean, in daylight, from all four sides plus the odometer, the registration book (partially covered) and any flaw you are pricing in. Disclose the flaws in the ad: the disclosed flaw is honesty; the discovered one is betrayal. In Vietnam's deep second-hand market, the bikes that move fastest at good prices are the transparent ones.

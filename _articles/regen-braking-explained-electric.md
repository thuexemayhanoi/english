Regenerative braking — "regen" — is the feature that most changes how an electric motorbike feels in traffic. It is also widely misunderstood, so here is what it actually does.

## The physics in one paragraph

An electric motor is reversible. When you roll off the throttle, the spinning wheel drives the motor as a generator, and the energy that would have burned off as brake heat flows back into the battery instead. The bike slows, the pack gains a little charge, and your brake pads do less work.

## How it feels on Vietnamese bikes

Dat Bike maps regen directly onto the throttle of the Weaver 200: ease off slightly and you slow gently; release fully and the bike slows firmly — the manufacturer describes controlling braking force through how much you release the grip. Riders in stop-start Hanoi traffic end up doing most speed modulation with the right hand, touching the hydraulic disc brakes only for full stops. Other brands implement regen as a fixed drag when the throttle closes, sometimes with modes.

## What regen is not

- **Not your full brake.** Regen acts on the drive wheel only, with limited force; the front disc still does the hard stopping. Emergency braking needs both controls.
- **Not a range miracle.** City riding recovers a modest percentage — helpful, not transformative. The big range levers remain speed and load.
- **Not reliable on ice-slick paint.** In the wet, strong regen can lock the rear momentarily; ease it progressively.

## Riding with regen well

Practise in a quiet street: feel the deceleration curve of your own bike, and learn which hand movement equals which braking force. Then in traffic you can ride a whole avenue without touching a lever — pads last longer, passengers feel smoother, and the pack recovers a little of every red light.

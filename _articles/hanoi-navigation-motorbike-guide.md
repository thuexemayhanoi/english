Hanoi has a reputation as unmappable, which is unfair: the city is extremely navigable once you understand how its addresses work and how its maps lie. Navigation is a skill, and like every skill in this city, it rewards ten minutes of understanding before your first ride.

## How Hanoi addresses work

A Vietnamese address runs from general to specific: street number, street name, ward, district, city. So "112 Nguyen Van Cu, Bo De, Long Bien, Ha Noi" means: Nguyen Van Cu street, in the Bo De ward of the Long Bien area. Two things surprise foreigners:

- **Numbers are not strictly sequential.** Street numbering exists but hopping occurs; numbers 40 and 112 may be a hundred metres apart in different blocks. Trust the number to confirm, not to navigate.
- **The ward/district layer is how people actually think.** "It's in Tay Ho" narrows a search better than a postcode — Vietnam's postal codes exist but almost nobody uses them day to day.

After the July 2025
 administrative restructuring, official ward names changed (Hanoi's former districts became 126 wards and communes), but every map, app and taxi conversation still runs on the old district names. Use them freely.

## App setup that matters

- **Use motorbike/ two-wheeler mode** in your navigation app. Car mode routes you onto car-priority roads and, worse, toward expressways and toll approaches that ban motorbikes entirely. Motorbike mode routes through the surface network the way riders actually move.
- **Trust the blue roads, verify the tiny grey ones.** Main arterials are mapped accurately. Small alleys ("ngo", "hem") appear inconsistently; the app may route you through a lane that is physically a corridor of parked bikes and hanging laundry. When the app wants to turn you into an alley, glance first — it is usually still possible, just slow.
- **Download offline maps** for Hanoi. Coverage is good but data drops in basements, under bridges and in the Old Quarter's concrete canyons.
- **Save your parking spots.** Dropping a pin when you park is the single most useful navigation habit in this city, which otherwise eats parked bikes into its alley maze.
- **Phone mount discipline**: a good mount keeps the phone at handlebar height; the phone-use rules are strict and riding while holding a phone is both fined and idiotic. Program the destination before you move; a glance per junction, not a stare.

## One-way reality

Hanoi's central grid is aggressively one-way, and the official direction data is only mostly right. The local riding culture includes tolerant short wrong-way movements on tiny streets, but as a visitor: do not ride against traffic on anything wider than a lane you can fully see. Follow the flow, accept the loop-around, and let the app recalculate rather than fighting it.

## Landmarks beat coordinates

The professional local technique is landmark stitching: ride from known point to known point — a bridge, a lake corner, a market, a monument. The city'
s skeleton (river, lakes, ring roads) makes this easy, which is why the district layout guide comes first in this cluster. Once Hoan Kiem, West Lake, the bridges and the ring roads are fixed in your head, every address in the city is "near something you know."

## The pre-ride minute

Before every non-trivial ride: check the route, note the bridge or the ring road it uses, screenshot the last 500 metres (the alley-level final approach is where navigation dies), and go. Hanoi rewards riders who look at the map before departure and ignore it once moving — the opposite of tourists, who stare at it while riding.

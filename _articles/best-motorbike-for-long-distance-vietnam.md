The best motorbike for long-distance riding in Vietnam is a reliable bike that your licence covers, with a tank range you have calculated and room for your luggage. On paved national roads that points to a 110-125 cc underbone or automatic scooter; a manual-clutch 150 cc bike suits only a rider whose licence covers it and who already rides manuals. This is general guidance: Nguyen Tu's support is primarily for Hanoi and local use, so ask the shop before booking whether a longer trip is possible.

Long-distance riding in Vietnam is a different discipline from city riding, and the bike is only one of the preparations. It gets first claim here.

## What long distance demands

- Sustained speed with margin: the default [motorbike speed limits]({{ '/articles/motorbike-speed-limits-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) outside residential areas are 70 km/h on dual carriageways and one-way roads with two or more lanes, and 60 km/h otherwise. A bike that cruises relaxed at that pace - not at its redline - is a bike that finishes the day without shaking your hands numb.
- Comfort: a real seat, an upright position, and ideally a windscreen for the highway sections.
- Reliability with a repair network: this is the underbone's hidden strength - Honda Wave-class bikes are simple and widespread, so parts and mechanics who know them are easier to find than for rarer models.
- Load capacity: luggage for days means a rack, bungees, and a bike that carries a passenger's worth of weight without drama.

### Engine size and power

Power decides whether a bike holds highway speed with something in hand or flat out. The figures below are the manufacturers' published values, retrieved on 23 September 2026. The larger bikes are included to show how the numbers change; this is not a list of what is available to rent, so contact the shop to confirm current availability of any model.

| Model | Engine | Published maximum power | Class on a new licence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Honda Wave Alpha 110 | 109.2 cc | 6.12 kW | A1 |
| Honda Wave RSX | 109.2 cc | 6.46 kW | A1 |
| Yamaha Sirius RC 110 | 110.3 cc | 5.9 kW | A1 |
| Honda Future 125 FI | 124.9 cc | 6.83 kW | A1 |
| Honda Vario 125 (scooter) | 125 cc | 8.48 kW | A1 |
| Yamaha Exciter 155 VVA (clutch) | 155 cc | 13.2 kW | A |

Read the table as a ladder of reserve. The four underbones publish between 5.9 and 6.83 kW and weights of 96 to 104 kg; the Exciter publishes 13.2 kW, close to double, at 123 kg (wet). A rider and a full bag add a load that can be comparable to the weight of an underbone itself, and reserve is spent on exactly that: long climbs, and the burst of speed needed to pass a truck on a national road.

More engine has costs. Above 125 cc the licence changes to class A, the bike is heavier, and a clutch bike asks more of a rider who will be shifting for hours at a time. A 125 cc scooter sits between the two, with 8.48 kW under an A1 licence; [scooter vs semi-automatic]({{ '/articles/scooter-vs-semi-automatic-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) sets out what changes for your hands and feet.

### Tank, fuel and the range sum

Range is two published numbers combined: tank size in litres divided by consumption in L/100 km, multiplied by 100. Manufacturers publish consumption under their own test conditions, and real-world consumption varies with speed, load, temperature and terrain, so treat the result as a planning ceiling.

| Model | Published tank | Published consumption | Calculated range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Honda Wave Alpha 110 | 3.7 L | 1.72 L/100 km | about 215 km |
| Yamaha Sirius RC 110 | 4.2 L | 2.08 L/100 km | about 202 km |
| Honda Wave RSX | 4.0 L | 1.56 L/100 km | about 256 km |
| Honda Future 125 FI | 4.6 L | 1.47 L/100 km | about 313 km |
| Honda Vario 125 (scooter) | 5.5 L | 2.11 L/100 km | about 261 km |
| Yamaha Exciter 155 VVA (clutch) | 5.4 L | 2.07 L/100 km | about 261 km |

Tank size and consumption matter more to range than engine size. The 109.2 cc Wave RSX reaches about 256 km on 4.0 L at 1.56 L/100 km, while the 155 cc Exciter 155 VVA reaches about 261 km on 5.4 L at 2.07 L/100 km, so the larger engine added almost no range. Use the table to see how the sum works, not to rank brands. For any other bike, take the tank and consumption from the manufacturer's page for that exact model and version and do the same sum.

Consumption is the number that moves most on a loaded bike in hills. Take an illustrative 4.0 L tank: at 2.0 L/100 km the sum gives 200 km, at 2.5 L/100 km it gives 160 km, and at 3.0 L/100 km about 133 km. Plan each fuel stop with a pessimistic consumption figure, refuel at the first sensible station rather than the last, and treat a small tank's gauge as a rough guide. The pacing and fuel stops of an underbone day are in [long-distance touring on a semi-automatic underbone]({{ '/articles/touring-semi-auto-underbone/' | relative_url }}).

### Luggage and load

Luggage changes how a bike handles before it changes how fast it goes. Pack heavy items low and close to the middle of the bike, keep nothing hanging from the handlebars, and check the straps at every stop.

Storage differs by type. Scooters publish an under-seat volume (23.2 L for the Honda Air Blade 125, 18 L for the Vario 125), while the Wave Alpha and Sirius RC pages record none, so luggage on those two lives on the rack. Rain cover for the bag matters on either.

### Comfort and braking

Comfort is mostly seat, posture and wind. A windscreen is an accessory, not something to assume on a rental bike, so ask at handover whether the bike has one; a gel pad can help with a hard seat, and regular rest stops matter as much as the seat.

Braking hardware differs by model and version, so judge the bike you are handed, not the model name. The Sirius RC page lists a front disc with a rear drum and notes that drum and disc versions exist; the Vario 125 lists a front disc with combined braking (CBS); the Exciter 155 lists front and rear discs, with ABS on limited ABS versions; the Wave Alpha page lists no disc version. On a long descent with luggage, brake in short stretches and, on a bike with gears, use a lower gear to hold speed rather than holding the lever; test both brakes loaded at handover.

## The honest machine ranking

1. Semi-automatic underbones (Wave, Sirius class): the touring reality of Vietnam. Slow, simple and widely serviced, with the easiest parts and repair logistics of the three.
2. Mid-size automatic scooters (Air Blade, Vision class): comfortable on good roads, best when the route sticks to paved highways.
3. Manual 125-150 cc and up: the right tool if your licence covers it and you already ride manuals - engine braking and power matter in the mountains.

The list is ordered by touring logistics (parts, repairs and load), not by speed or comfort. Ask the shop what outside repair is acceptable before any serious work is done. ## The licence and law layer

Bigger bikes need bigger licence classes: under the 2025 law an [A1 licence covers up to 125 cc]({{ '/articles/a1-licence-vietnam-what-you-can-ride/' | relative_url }}) (older A1 licences stay valid for bikes below 175 cc) and the [A class]({{ '/articles/vietnam-a-licence-what-you-can-ride/' | relative_url }}) covers larger bikes. Riding long distance unlicensed multiplies exposure - hours on the road, multiple provinces, checkpoint probability. And [motorbikes are banned from the expressways]({{ '/articles/motorbikes-on-expressway-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) entirely; long-distance riders take the old national highways, which is part of the experience.

The line that matters for a rental is the one in the power table. Under Article 57 of Law 36/2024/QH15, as set out in the licence guides above, a new A1 covers petrol bikes up to and including 125 cc and electric bikes up to 11 kW, and class A covers everything above those limits. An A1 issued before 1 January 2025 keeps its older scope under Article 89, which is why an older A1 can cover petrol bikes below 175 cc. A visitor's position depends on the home licence plus an international driving permit that includes a motorcycle category. Whatever the class, [the documents to carry while riding]({{ '/articles/documents-to-carry-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) go with you on every day of the trip.

## What to ask the shop before a longer trip

The shop's published support is primarily for Hanoi and local use. For a trip beyond that, state the route, the provinces, the number of days and the day you plan to bring the bike back, and ask whether that trip is possible before you book. Nothing on this page describes a one-way rental, a drop-off in another city, roadside rescue or a touring package, and none of them should be assumed.

Points worth raising in that conversation:

- **Late return.** The published late-return charge is 20,000 VND per hour, and more than 6 hours late a full additional rental day may be charged (typically 150,000-200,000 VND depending on the motorcycle). A ride that can meet bad weather or a breakdown needs a buffer day in the plan, not a tight return time.
- **Maintenance and consumables.** The shop's maintenance may include an oil change, chain adjustment, tyre-pressure checks and a normal inspection, while inner tubes and tyres may be the renter's responsibility depending on the situation. Ask who pays for a puncture repair on the road.
- **Checks on a long rental.** The owner's practical guidance is periodic checks around 1,500 km or more of use. It is owner guidance, not a manufacturer interval, so ask how a check would fit your route.
- **Insurance.** Nguyen Tu does not provide motorbike insurance; customers arrange their own. For a multi-province trip, read your own policy to see whether it covers a rented motorcycle on those roads.

## Checks that matter on a multi-day ride

The inspection checklist covers the handover. These are the additions once days and kilometres stack up.

1. **A loaded shakedown ride.** Ride a short loop near Hanoi with the luggage strapped on before committing to the route. Faults that stay hidden in city riding, such as vibration or fuel starvation, can show up at sustained speed.
2. **Tyres set for the load.** Check tread and sidewalls, and set pressures from the figures for that bike rather than a guess.
3. **Chain and oil each morning.** Check chain slack, lubrication and oil level before the day starts. After each fuel stop, walk around the bike and tug the straps.
4. **Lights, horn and mirrors.** A long day can end after dark, so check them before you leave.
5. **Documents and phone.** Carry your licence, and the permit where one is needed. Ask at handover how the registration certificate and the compulsory insurance certificate will be shown at a stop ([what a rider must carry]({{ '/articles/documents-to-carry-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }})), and keep the shop's number on a charged phone.
6. **Day length.** Plan by the road, not the map, and check the posted limit on each road, because a sign overrides the default.

## Questions about long-distance bikes

### Which licence do I need for a bike bigger than 125 cc?

On a new Vietnamese licence, class A. A new A1 stops at 125 cc and an A1 issued before 2025 keeps its older scope below 175 cc, while a visitor's position depends on the home licence plus a permit that includes a motorcycle category. The licence section above has the detail.

### Is a 110 cc bike enough for a long trip?

It can be, if you accept its limits. The published power of 5.9 to 6.83 kW leaves little reserve for climbs with luggage, so plan shorter days and more stops; riders who want more reserve should look at 125 cc and above within their licence.

### Can I take a Hanoi rental to Hue or round the Ha Giang loop?

That is a question for the shop. Its support is primarily for Hanoi and local use, so ask before booking whether a longer trip is possible.

### Can a bigger bike use the expressway?

No. Motorbikes of every class are barred from expressways regardless of engine size; the [expressway guide]({{ '/articles/motorbikes-on-expressway-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) has the rule.

Dogs sleep in the middle of warm asphalt, buffalo wander without supervision, chickens conduct family business across rural roads, and pigs travel unannounced. In city streets the animal hazard is small and slow; on rural highways it is the difference between a normal ride and a flight over the handlebars.

## Read the animal, not just the road

Dogs mostly move toward or away from their own home territory; one trotting along the verge will usually keep going, but one standing alert on the shoulder is a coin flip. Cattle and buffalo are slow but utterly unpredictable about which side of the road they prefer. Slow down early for any large animal: swerving at speed is far deadlier than braking — the sequence in emergency-braking-motorbike-vietnam is what you will actually use.

## The horn buys you a decision

A beep far enough in advance gives a dog time to make its choice while you still have braking distance left. Beeping at the last second startles the animal into your path — timing is everything.

## Where animals cluster

Village entrances, market roads, house-fronts at dusk and the shade under trees are animal hotspots. So are rivers: buffalo are walked to water morning and evening. On rural stretches, scan the verges and open gates the way you would scan junctions, and cover the brake anywhere a driveway, farm track or open field meets the road.

## When the surface adds to the problem

Rural roads add gravel and mud to the animal equation: any emergency maneuver has less grip than you hope — gravel-roads-motorbike-vietnam covers riding loose surfaces, and riding-downhill-motorbike-engine-braking the extra care when animals appear on slopes.

## If a collision is unavoidable

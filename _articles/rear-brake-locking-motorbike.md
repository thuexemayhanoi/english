A rear brake that locks with almost no warning is a genuine hazard: on a wet Vietnamese road, a locked wheel skids instantly, and skids fall over. Drum brakes — still the standard rear brake on most local bikes — are simple, and their locking causes are all simple too.

## The difference between bite and lock

A healthy drum brake is progressive: pressure rises, braking rises. A brake that goes from nothing to locked within a few millimetres of pedal travel has lost its modulation, and modulation is the entire game. Causes, in order of frequency:

## Worn shoes and the self-adjuster story

Drum shoes wear gradually, and many drums rely on adjusters — automatic or a simple threaded rod — to keep the bite point near the top of the pedal. Worn shoes past their limit, or a corroded, lazy self-adjuster, leave a long pedal... until the cam suddenly goes over centre and the shoe jams hard against the drum: that is the sudden lock. Check the pedal free play per the manual (part of brake-checks-motorbike-vietnam) and have the shoes measured when the drum is open; shoe thickness limits are a manual figure, not an estimate.

## Corroded cam and return springs

The actuating cam — the little eccentric that spreads the shoes — corrodes in humid air and after flooding. A rusty cam sticks in the applied position: the brake drags, heats, then locks on its own at the next application. Weak or broken return springs produce the same refusal to release. Inside a drum, this is a strip-and-clean job: clean the cam, grease only the specified points sparingly (grease on friction surfaces ruins braking), replace tired springs.

## Cable and linkage drag

Rear drum brakes are usually cable or rod operated, and a cable that drags inside a rusted sleeve holds the brake partly applied: the drum heats, the drum expands with heat against the shoes, and the cycle ends in a lock-up, usually in traffic, usually hot. A fully free pedal return — snapping back on its own — is the test; if not, clean and lubricate the cable or replace it. The dragging-brake logic in general is in sticking-brakes-caliper-cleaning.

## Water and the wet-drum moment

After deep puddles or flooding, a wet drum loses friction, then recovers unevenly as it dries: an application early in a ride can be weak, and a minute later, grabby. Riding the first few wet applications gently, testing braking before you need it, is the discipline; the wet-road risk picture is in after-rain-slippery-roads-motorbike.

## The lock itself

If the rear locks while riding: look up, keep the bars straight, ease off the brake smoothly, and let the wheel spin back up. Slamming the front brake while the rear skids converts one problem into a crash. Practice controlled braking in a safe empty area — the technique of braking at the limit belongs to the riding-safety guides, but the machine-side fix is what this article owes you: a brake that locks predictably at the same pedal position, every time.

Carrying a pillion changes almost every input on a manual-clutch motorbike, and the clutch is where the change is felt first. Launches that were easy solo now need more revs and a slower lever release; stops that were routine now need more distance. The technique itself is simple — the discipline is in adjusting every habit.

## Brief your passenger first

Before you ride: pillion helmet on, feet on the pillion pegs once moving, hands on the grab rail or around your waist, knees against the bike, lean with the bike, and no sudden weight shifts. Agree that they mount only after you say so, feet down, engine off or clutch held. A passenger who leans against a corner or shifts their weight at a traffic-light launch is the biggest cause of wobbles.

## Launching two-up

With roughly a passenger's worth of extra weight, the engine needs more revs before you release the clutch — noticeably more, not a little more. Let the lever out a shade slower than solo, and do not aim for fast getaways. The mistake most riders make is under-revving, feeling the bike labour, then adding a fistful of throttle mid-launch — that lurches the pillion backwards and destabilises the front. Set the revs first, release smoothly, and let the bike accelerate gently.

## Clutch work in traffic

Slipping distances grow: with two people aboard you will ride the friction zone more often and longer in slow traffic, because the bike responds more slowly to every input. Give junctions one extra metre of space. Cover the clutch early on approach so you are never caught flat-footed in the wrong gear — second gear two-up behaves much like first gear solo at the same speed.

## Braking and downshifting

More weight means longer stopping distances, so brake earlier and finish slower. Downshift one gear sooner than you would solo, and release the clutch more gently between gears — a quick lever release with a pillion sends a jerk through the whole drivetrain and the passenger's helmet into yours. Smoothness is the whole game.

## Slow-speed balance

At walking pace, a pillion's weight exaggerates every wobble. Keep their weight low and centred: if your passenger sits tall and leans around corners, the bike fights you both. Where possible, avoid long creeping sections two-up; walking pace with a passenger is advanced-level clutch work, and it is fine to put a foot down and walk the bike instead.

## Who should ride two-up

Only carry a passenger once your solo clutch control is genuinely solid — friction-zone drills, clean launches every time, no stalls.

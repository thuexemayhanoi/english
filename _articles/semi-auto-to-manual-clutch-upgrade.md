Semi-automatic riders already shift with their feet — the upgrade to a full manual clutch is smaller than it looks, but the changes are real.

## What stays the same

The gear lever logic (down for lower, up for higher), the brake layout, the seating position, and all your road craft. If you can ride a Wave or a Sirius smoothly in traffic, you already shift by feel; that feel transfers directly.

## What changes

- A clutch lever appears on the left bar, and you must use it for every shift — no more clutchless clicks.
- Neutral moves from the bottom of the pattern to between first and second.
- Stalling becomes possible. A semi-automatic cannot stall; a manual can, and will, until the launch becomes muscle memory.
- The engine disconnects whenever you pull the lever, so coasting downhill is now a choice you must refuse.

## Habits to unlearn

Semi-automatics reward aggressive, instant shifting — click whenever you like. On a manual, every click needs a lever squeeze and a smooth release, so the rhythm becomes: throttle off, clutch in, click, clutch out, throttle on. Slower, but deliberate. Semi-auto riders also tend to brake to a full stop in whatever gear they were in; on a manual you downshift through the gears as you slow, arriving in first ready to launch.

## Why upgrade at all

A manual clutch lets you hold torque exactly where you want it — launches, overtakes, descents — and the sport underbones that use it (Winner R, Exciter 155) are the performance end of the underbone class. Our who-needs-a-manual-150 guide covers whether the upgrade matches your actual riding; for most city commuters, a semi-automatic remains the smarter tool.

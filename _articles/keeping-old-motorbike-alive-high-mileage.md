Vietnam runs on high-mileage motorbikes: bikes that have crossed 50,000, 80,000 kilometres and keep serving. Old bikes are honest — everything wrong with them is knowable, and every part is available. The skill of keeping one alive is not mechanical talent; it is a spending strategy. Fix what stops you, prevent what strands you, ignore what is merely cosmetic.

## Know what you own

An old bike's health is measured, not guessed: compression (the tired-engine signs are in low-compression-signs-motorbike), oil consumption, brake wear, chain stretch, bearing play. A one-time thorough assessment by a good mechanic — the finding-one guide is motorbike-service-where-vietnam — turns "an old bike" into a list: what is worn, what is dying, what is fine. That list is the budget.

## The priority ladder

1. **Safety items, always.** Brakes, tyres, steering. An old bike with perfect brakes is a modest, safe vehicle; a fast old bike with tired brakes is a scheduled accident.
2. **Stranding items.** The parts whose failure ends a journey: battery, chain, clutch cable, plugs, fuel lines. Cheap, replaced before failure, carried as spares on long rides — spare-parts-kit-underseat-motorbike.
3. **Engine-life items.** Oil above all: an old engine lives on clean oil at shortened intervals. Filters, valve clearances on schedule, air filter in a dusty city.
4. **Comfort and cosmetics, last.** Rattly panels, a scuffed fairing, a sagging seat: fix them only when the ladder above is fully current.

## The two rules of old-bike economics

**Fix wear, not age.** Replace parts that are measurably worn; do not "renew" things for the feeling of it. The decision framework per part is in repair-or-replace-motorbike-parts.

**Do not chase perfection.** A high-mileage engine that starts, doesn't smoke badly, and holds oil is *working*. Spending a new engine's cost restoring an old one to showroom condition is a hobby decision, not a transport decision — and it is legitimate only when you know which one you are making. The honest data for that decision: measured compression, oil consumption between changes, and the noise map in diagnosing-motorbike-noises-vietnam.

## The habits that extend everything

- Fresh oil at short intervals — the single cheapest life-extender.
- Listen after every ride: new noises get named early, while they are cheap.
- Keep fasteners tight: old bikes rattle themselves apart; vibration loosens, looseness widens holes, widened holes end frames.
- Wash modestly, dry carefully — corrosion is the old bike's slow enemy; motorcycle-rust-prevention-vietnam.
- Fix leaks when they appear: oil leaks kill more old engines by running them dry than by anything else — oil-leaks-motorbike-finding-fixing.

## When to stop

An old bike earns retirement when the safety ladder can no longer be kept current for sensible money, or when the frame, engine or wiring have all reached end-of-life together. Until then, the old-bike strategy is steady, boring and effective: correct oil, working brakes, measured wear, and spending in the order above. The Vietnamese fleet is proof the strategy works — most of it is running on it.

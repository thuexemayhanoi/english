Rain changes the surface under your tyre and the traction available to your rear wheel. On a manual bike the clutch becomes your traction management tool: it lets you feed power in a way a snapped-open throttle never can. Wet-weather clutch technique is about one idea — everything slower, everything smoother.

## Launch on wet paint, mud and leaves

The first metres after a downpour are the slickest: wet road markings, mud dragged onto tarmac at construction sites, and leaves in the gutters. Launch with slightly more revs than a dry, gentle start needs, and release the clutch a touch slower, so the engine never receives a sudden shove of resistance. If the rear wheel spins or the bike squats and shudders, hold the lever still for half a second — the slip absorbs the shock — then continue releasing. Never respond to wheelspin by chopping the throttle; ease the clutch back in instead.

## Ride the friction zone through standing water

Through flooded stretches and across painted arrows at junctions, keep the clutch in the friction zone and the speed steady, with the throttle barely open. Partial slip means the rear tyre receives a fraction of the engine's force — exactly what you want when the tyre's grip is halved. The same technique works on the wet metal of bridge expansion joints and the greasy centre of your lane after days of dry weather.

## Shift timing in the wet

Change gears a little earlier than you would in the dry, at lower revs, and release the lever more gently. A hard, fast clutch release under wet conditions is a small wheelspin generator. On descents, avoid abrupt engine-braking grabs: downshift early, feed the clutch in progressively.

## Braking mindset

Wet-weather braking is a braking topic more than a clutch topic, but the two interact: the engine braking you get from releasing the clutch in a low gear is rear-wheel braking, and on a slick surface that can lock the rear more easily than in the dry. Downshift smoothly, brake with both brakes earlier and gentler, and leave triple the usual following distance.

## After the rain stops

The first thirty minutes of a dry-off are still slippery — dust and oil lifted by the rain sit on top of the tarmac. Keep the wet-weather clutch discipline until the road looks properly dry again, and remember that in the dry season the first rain after weeks of dust is the most dangerous road state of all.

Manual bikes reward this patience: riders who learn friction-zone rain technique stall less and slide less.

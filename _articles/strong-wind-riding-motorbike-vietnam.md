Wind is the most underestimated riding hazard in Vietnam — invisible until a gust shoves you half a metre sideways at 60 km/h. It matters most where the geography opens up: bridges, rice-field roads, mountain passes and coastal stretches.

## Where gusts come from

Trucks and buses push a bow wave of air as they pass, then suck you toward them as they clear. Expect it: loosen your grip slightly, keep your elbows bent and lean a touch into the passing vehicle. Bridges and embankments funnel wind — open Vietnamese bridges can hold a steady crosswind the entire span. See riding-motorbike-blind-spots-vietnam for the other side of passing heavy vehicles.

## Crosswind technique

Ride slightly into the wind so the bike holds a straight line, and expect the sheltered moment when you pass behind a truck or a wall — the wind returns abruptly on the other side. Hold the tank with your knees, keep speed steady, and avoid sudden throttle or brake inputs: smoothness is grip in wind. A windshield or a box on the back catches wind; remove the box if it makes the bike nervous.

## Wind plus rain plus light bikes

Small underbones and scooters — light wheels, high profiles — get moved around more than heavy bikes. If strong wind is forecast and your route is open, consider a heavier machine; best-motorbike-for-long-distance-vietnam covers that choice for open-road travel. Mountain passes add their own turbulence around ridgelines: mountain-pass-riding-motorbike-vietnam covers them.

## When to stop

If the bike cannot hold a steady line and the gusts keep winning, that is not a technique problem — it is a parking problem. Find shelter on the leeward side of a building and wait; Vietnamese weather fronts usually pass within hours.

## The one-line version

Loose grip, steady throttle, lean into the gust, respect trucks and open bridges — and when the wind wins, let it.

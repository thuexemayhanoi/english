The engine that pulls cleanly in town but starves on the highway — the revs build, then flatten, then the bike feels like it is running on wishes — is the signature of fuel starvation. The engine is asking for more fuel than the system can deliver. It is common in Vietnam, and the causes line up almost perfectly from cheapest to most expensive.

## Why it shows at high revs

At low speeds the engine sips; at high revs it drinks. A partially blocked filter, a kinked line or a weak flow can keep a pot of tea warm but not a kettle at full boil. So the bike idles fine, commutes fine, and starves exactly when overtaking at highway speed — the most dangerous moment a fault can choose.

## The ladder, cheapest first

- **Fuel level and the tap.** Obvious but real: a nearly empty tank, or a tap left on RESERVE after refilling, or a partially blocked tap strainer. The tap's behaviour and logic are in fuel-tap-reserve-motorbike.
- **The fuel filter.** A clogged filter flows enough for idle, not for full throttle. Filters are cheap and visible — check the direction arrow when refitting, and inspect the element for rust (which points upstream to fuel-tank-rust-prevention-treatment). The full filter-and-line guide is fuel-filters-fuel-lines-motorbike.
- **Fuel lines pinched or old.** A line collapsed internally by age looks fine outside and flows like a straw in a milkshake. After any recent service, check that a line was not re-routed against the frame.
- **The tank vent.** Tanks breathe through a vent; a blocked vent builds vacuum as fuel drains until flow stops — the tell-tale is a bike that dies at speed and restarts after opening the fuel cap with a whoosh.
- **The carburettor's main circuit or the injector.** Grit or gum in the main jet limits exactly the top end; the cleaning procedure is carburettor-cleaning-tuning-vietnam and the injector-side story is fuel-injector-cleaning-vietnam.
- **The fuel pump, on injected bikes.** A weakening pump delivers idle fuel but not full-load fuel. Diagnosing pumps honestly needs pressure measurement — mechanic territory.

## The safe response at speed

When power flattens on a highway: do not fight it — hold your lane, ease off to a speed the engine accepts, and pull over when safe. Starvation feels unnervingly like an engine about to seize; the difference is that it recovers when the demand drops. Once parked, open the fuel cap slowly and listen: a long hiss of inrushing air convicts the vent.

## Prevention

Fresh filters on schedule, a tank kept clean and full (water and rust both narrow fuel passages — water-in-fuel-motorbike), and petrol from busy stations. Fuel starvation is the rare fault that almost always announces itself gently first — a subtle top-end softness — and listening to the gentle version is the whole trick. Its opposite twin, the engine that runs rich and merely wastes money, is covered in improving-fuel-economy-motorbike.

A GPS tracker is the only security device that keeps working after the bike is gone. Locks stop the theft from starting; a tracker tells you where it went. In Vietnamese cities where motorbikes vanish into alley networks and get re-registered in other provinces, that difference is the whole product.

## How they work

A tracker contains a GPS receiver (where the bike is), a cellular modem (how it tells you), and a power connection or battery. You watch it through an app; good ones report on movement, ignition, and a geofence breach (the bike leaving an area you defined). Subscription models pay for the SIM data; battery-only models work for objects, not vehicles — a bike tracker needs a wired power source, ideally with its own small backup battery so cutting it triggers an alert instead of silence.

## Installation realities

Wiring a tracker means tapping into the bike’s 12V system — a job for someone competent, badly spliced electrics cause the faults described in wiring-connector thinking; if the bike is a rental, that is an unapproved modification, so ask first (the general rules are in rental agreements — motorbike-rental-agreement-hanoi context). Placement matters: hidden, so a thief cannot find and discard it in the first five minutes, but not so buried that the antenna loses signal. A tracker is a complement, not a substitute: a visible disc lock makes the opportununist walk away in the first place — disc-locks-chains-motorbike-security — and an alarm adds the noise layer (motorbike-alarms-vietnam).

## The honest limits

Trackers fail silently: dead SIM, dead battery, dead coverage. Test monthly. A tracker helps recovery, but recovery is not guaranteed and the police report is still your responsibility — the theft process and your obligations, especially on a rental, are in theft-loss-responsibility-rental-motorbike-hanoi and keeping-rental-motorbike-secure-hanoi. And do not go confront a thief yourself; the tracker’s job ends at giving the location to the people who handle that part.

Crossing half of Vietnam on a Wave-class underbone is a normal Vietnamese thing to do — families ride them from Hanoi to their home provinces every Tet. The format is light, simple, fuel-cheap and fixable in every village. What it is not is fast, wind-protected or armchair-comfortable. Tour on one successfully by designing the trip around what it is.

## Pacing: the 250 km day

Plan days around 200 to 300 kilometres, with a real break every 100 to 150. A small air-cooled engine at 40 to 55 km/h cruises all day; the same engine pinned at its maximum for hours is how touring trips end in repair shops. Vietnam's secondary roads reward slow travel anyway — the distance is the point, not the arrival time. A useful habit: after every fuel stop, walk around the bike, check the chain visually, tug the luggage straps.

## Fuel and range

Semi-auto underbones sip fuel, but tanks are small — commonly under five litres — so range can be under 200 km. In remote areas do not pass a working petrol station with the gauge under half. Empty stretches exist in the northern mountains, and "the next station" there can be a long way down the road.

## Luggage

Pack light, pack low, pack strapped: the cargo guide covers placement and tie-downs in detail. For touring, the tried-and-true setup is a rear bag on the rack secured by a net, valuables in a small backpack worn on the chest rather than the back (better balance, better in the heat), and nothing on the bars. Rain cover for the luggage even in the dry season — an afternoon mountain storm is a when, not an if.

## Comfort and weather

- **Wind and fatigue**: at underbone speeds, wind protection barely exists. A cheap pair of glasses or a helmet visor saves streaming eyes; earplugs reduce drone fatigue on long days.
- **Heat**: ride mornings, rest at noon, ride again late afternoon — the local pattern. Hydrate more than feels necessary.
- **Cold mountains**: the northern winter passes are genuinely cold on an unfaired bike; layers beat a single thick jacket.
- **Night**: finish riding before dark where you can; unlit rural roads with buffalo and trucks are a different risk class.

## Where the format shines

Village mechanics everywhere know these engines; parts are universal; fuel costs are tiny; and a breakdown rarely strands you for long. The touring trade-offs are honest: no speed reserve, modest comfort, and vibration over long days. Riders who accept them see more of Vietnam at the speed it actually lives at.

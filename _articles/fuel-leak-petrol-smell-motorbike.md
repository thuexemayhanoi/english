A petrol smell on or after a ride is never a personality trait of the bike — it is fuel somewhere it should not be, and unlike most maintenance issues, this one carries a fire risk. Stop riding and start looking.

## Where the smell comes from

The fuel line and its connections, the carburettor overflow, a perished tap or tank seal, or worst of all a leaking tank seam. On carburetted bikes — the majority of Vietnam's fleet, see carburettor-vs-fuel-injection-vietnam — a sticking float makes the carb overflow and drip fuel, often visible as a wet, shimmering patch under the bike after parking. Fuel-injected bikes leak at connections or injector seals instead.

## The check sequence

Engine off, bike standing on level ground: look under the tank, along the fuel line to the carburettor, and under the engine for drips or wet lines. Run a finger along the line — wetness means fuel. Check whether the smell appears only after filling up (a sloppy filler neck seal) or only when the tap is on (the line or carb). A visible, active drip is a do-not-ride condition; a wet line is a do-not-ride-today-and-fix-it condition, because a leak that is only a smell in the morning can become a spray on a bumpy road.

## The fire logic

Petrol vapour ignites easily, engines get hot, and electrical systems spark. That combination is why fuel leaks are treated as emergencies rather than annoyances, and why you park a leaking bike away from open flames and cigarette smokers — including the inevitable cafe's kitchen door.

## Fixes and rentals

A cracked fuel line is a five-minute, very cheap fix at any shop — motorbike-service-where-vietnam.

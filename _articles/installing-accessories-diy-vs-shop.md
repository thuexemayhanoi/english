You have the accessory in your hands and a choice to make: spanner in the driveway, or bike to the shop. In Vietnam labour is cheap and mechanics are everywhere, which changes the calculus from Western assumptions — but so does the value of knowing your own machine.

## The DIY tier

Genuinely bolt-on, zero-risk-if-reversible items: phone mounts, tank bags, seat pads, bottle holders, covers, mirror swaps, bulbs, grips. If it needs only basic tools (motorbike-rider-toolkit-essentials) and attaches to existing fasteners, doing it yourself is free education: every accessory you fit yourself is a fastener you will know how to check later, which matters in a country where vibration loosens everything. Follow the torque-by-feel discipline and check after the first ride.

## The shop tier

Anything that drills, splices, welds, or unbolts structure: electrical wiring (chargers, alarms, heated grips — the humidity and bad-splice failure modes are described in wiring-connectors-humidity-motorbike), racks and top-boxes mounted to frames, crash bars, skid plates, suspension, and every brake-adjacent part. The failure modes here are not "it falls off" but "it fails at speed". Vietnamese workshop rates for this work are modest, and a good shop does it right in twenty minutes.

## The middle tier — judge honestly
<arg_value>Chain and sprocket work, variator rollers, forks: technically within reach of a careful amateur, but the tool list grows and the cost of error rises with each. The learn-the-basics path is learning-basic-motorbike-maintenance — start with the maintenance, graduate to the accessories. And when you do use a shop, agree the scope and price first, the way repair-quotes-parts-pricing-vietnam teaches; accessory installs deserve quotes too.

## The meta-skill

Knowing which tier a job belongs to is itself the skill: it decides which failures you cause yourself. The repair conversation basics in talking-to-mechanic-vietnam cover the rest.

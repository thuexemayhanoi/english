Even a planned city ride meets gravel: construction access roads, the final kilometre to a village homestay, roadworks, and the sand that rain washes across corners. Riding loose surfaces is a skill worth having before you need it.

## What changes

Grip drops sharply on loose gravel: braking distances stretch, the front wheel washes out easily, and the rear slides under power. Small-diameter scooter wheels sink and wander more than larger wheels. Momentum becomes your friend — a steady speed floats over the surface, while abrupt inputs dig in.

## The technique

Weight back slightly, arms loose, look far ahead. Brake with the rear more than the front — hard front-brake use is the classic gravel fall. Slow down before gravel, not on it, and corner by steering gently with the whole body rather than leaning. Avoid mid-gravel direction changes; pick a line early and hold it smoothly. ## Where Vietnam hides gravel

Construction sites spill gravel onto main roads for weeks. Rural bridges' approach ramps hold deep loose stones. Mountain passes scatter fallen rock — mountain-pass-riding-motorbike-vietnam covers those roads — and after heavy rain, sand fans across corners at the bottom of slopes. Treat any corner on a rural descent as potentially gravelly until proven otherwise.

## Deep gravel and mud

If the surface is deep — wheel-deep stones or wet clay mud — the honest answer is to walk the bike through or turn around. Scooters especially are at their limit in deep mud: belly-pan clearance is tiny and the CVT intake sits low. For day trips that will include rural roads, plan the bike accordingly; best-motorbike-for-day-trips-from-hanoi has that comparison.

## The one-line version

Slow before, steady through, rear brake, weight back — and when gravel becomes mud, walking the bike is the skilled choice, not the defeat.

Three transmissions, one city. The choice is easier than it looks because each transmission answers a different kind of rider. Automatic, semi-automatic or manual, the short answer is this: take an automatic scooter if you have never ridden or are new to Vietnam, a semi-automatic underbone if you have ridden before and are happy to shift with your foot, and a manual-clutch bike only if you ride one at home.

## The three types and what Hanoi calls them

Honda Vietnam's motorbike range page offers the categories Xe tay ga, Xe số and Xe côn tay, alongside Xe phân khối lớn (big bikes) and Xe Điện (electric). Those three labels match the three transmissions in this guide: xe tay ga is the automatic scooter, xe so the semi-automatic underbone and xe con tay the manual-clutch bike. Knowing the Vietnamese words helps when a sign, a listing or another rider uses them instead of the English ones.

| Look at | Automatic scooter | Semi-automatic underbone | Manual-clutch bike |
|---|---|---|---|
| Vietnamese name | xe tay ga | xe so | xe con tay |
| Left hand | brake lever | no lever | clutch lever |
| Left foot | nothing to do | gear lever | gear lever |
| Gear changes | none to make | by foot, no clutch lever | by foot, with the clutch lever |
| In the published rate list | Vision, Click, Air Blade, Mio | Wave, Sirius | none listed |

"Semi-automatic" is the label that misleads, because it can sound like an automatic with extras. Here it means a bike with gears but no clutch lever: the rider picks the gear with the left foot and an automatic clutch does the rest.

### Telling them apart at the counter

Look at the left foot first. A gear lever there means the bike is not an automatic scooter, even though an underbone has a step-through frame like a scooter's and is easy to mistake for one. Then look at the left grip: on a bike with a gear lever, a lever on the grip is a clutch lever (manual), and no lever means semi-automatic.

Be careful with the word manual, because it can be taken to mean any bike with a gear lever or only a bike with a clutch lever. Ask the direct question: is there a clutch lever on the left grip? If the bike has gears, also ask to be shown the gear pattern before you ride off.

## Automatic scooters (Honda Vision, Click, Air Blade, Yamaha Mio)

Twist and go. No gears, no clutch, no left hand doing anything but the brake. The transmission takes no learning: the controls are the throttle and the two brake levers. In stop-start Hanoi traffic the automatic is a genuine safety margin - all your attention goes to the road, none to a gearbox. Under-seat storage for helmets and shopping. The trade-offs: slightly higher rental rate on some models, smaller wheels than an underbone on rough roads, and a drivetrain that is less self-serviceable in the provinces.

Honda Vietnam lists the Vision and the Air Blade 125 as automatic (CVT), a transmission with no fixed gear steps. On wheels, Honda gives the Vision a 16-inch front wheel, while the Wave Alpha 110 and the Sirius RC 110 are listed with 17-inch tyres (70/90-17 at the front). As general guidance, a larger wheel copes better with potholes and kerb edges.

The one skill an automatic asks for is a smooth throttle hand. With no gear to hold the bike back, a sharp twist from a standstill sends it forward quickly, so open the throttle progressively at every green light and keep a finger on a brake lever while you wait. ## Semi-automatic underbones (Honda Wave, Yamaha Sirius)

Locally this is the xe so. Foot-shifted four-speed gearbox, but no hand clutch - the gearbox does it. Compact, cheap to run, and simple enough that roadside mechanics are used to working on them. The trade-off: a small learning curve for the foot-shift, and storage means a bag on the rack.

Honda lists a mechanical four-speed rotary gearbox for the Wave Alpha 110, and Yamaha lists a four-speed rotary gearbox with a wet multi-plate centrifugal clutch for the Sirius RC 110. A centrifugal clutch engages by itself as engine speed rises, which is why there is no lever and why the stalling a clutch bike can suffer when pulling away does not arise in the same way.

Do not expect a large saving in weight over a scooter such as the Vision: the published weights of the Wave Alpha 110 (96 kg), the Vision (97 kg) and the Sirius RC 110 (100 kg wet) are within a few kilograms of each other. [The Old Quarter bike guide]({{ '/articles/best-bike-for-hanoi-old-quarter/' | relative_url }}) has the full size table, widths included.

The skill to learn is the foot-shift rhythm: ease off the throttle, click, then open the throttle again, and click down towards first as you slow so that you are in the right gear to pull away. Closed shoes make this easier than open sandals, because the left foot works the lever. ## Manual (clutch) motorcycles

Left-hand clutch, left-foot shift, full control. For riders who already ride manuals, this is home, and a manual is the right tool for mountain trips where engine braking matters. For everyone else it is the hardest possible way to learn Vietnamese traffic - the clutch turns every junction into a workload. Rent a manual only if you ride one at home.

Locally this is the xe con tay. The rider squeezes the clutch lever to disconnect the engine from the gearbox for every gear change and every stop, and releases it gradually to pull away. Release it too fast and the engine stalls. The narrow band where the bike begins to move is called the friction zone, and finding it again and again among close traffic is the skill that takes practice.

Manual-clutch bikes are not in the published rate list further down, so ask about a specific bike and its rate. The [manual-clutch guides]({{ '/topics/manual-clutch/' | relative_url }}) cover how the format works and how to practise, and [how to ride a manual-clutch motorbike in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/how-to-ride-manual-clutch-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) is the place to start. ## What Hanoi traffic asks of each

Hanoi riding is a long series of small demands: stopping and starting with bikes close on every side, creeping through jams and slipping through gaps that are only a bike wide. The three transmissions meet those demands differently.

### Pulling away at lights and junctions

- Automatic: nothing to select. Roll to a stop on both brakes, then open the throttle smoothly when the light changes.
- Semi-automatic: click down as you slow so that you stop in first gear. Pulling away in a higher gear is sluggish and makes the clutch work harder.
- Manual: stop with the lever pulled in, and find neutral if the wait is long. The first pull-away after green is the moment to concentrate, because a stall there leaves riders close behind.

### Gaps, crawling and filtering

In a jam the whole flow crawls, and the gaps open and close in a moment. An automatic uses throttle and brakes alone, and both feet are free to touch down. A semi-automatic crawls in first gear with no lever to manage, though it cannot give the fine low-speed control of a skilled clutch rider.

A clutch bike gives the most control at walking pace to a rider who has mastered the friction zone, and demands the most from one who has not. ## The decision

- Never ridden, or riding in Vietnam for the first time: automatic. Full stop.
- Ridden before, want the cheapest and simplest: semi-automatic.
- Confident manual rider with proper licence coverage: manual, and only if your routes leave the city.

The cases in between are in this table.

| Your situation | Start with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| You ride a scooter at home and are new to Vietnam | Automatic | The traffic is the new skill; the gearbox can wait |
| You ride a clutch bike at home and will stay in the city | Automatic or semi-automatic | A clutch adds work at every junction, and city riding gives the extra control little to do |
| You want to learn a clutch while in Hanoi | Automatic or semi-automatic first | Learn the traffic before adding the clutch |
| You are staying a few days | Automatic | There is little time to learn anything but the traffic |

Think of the three types as a ladder of workload and climb it in order. ## Licence class follows engine size

One legal note: in Vietnam, licence class, not transmission, decides what you may ride - the A1 class covers the common rental sizes; bigger manuals need the A class. Check what your licence actually covers before renting anything with a clutch.

The classes are written in terms of engine size, electric power and number of wheels, not gearbox. As [the A1 licence guide]({{ '/articles/a1-licence-vietnam-what-you-can-ride/' | relative_url }}) and [the licence classes guide]({{ '/articles/vietnam-motorbike-licence-classes-explained/' | relative_url }}) set out, class A1 under Article 57 of Law 36/2024/QH15 covers two-wheel petrol motorbikes up to and including 125cc and electric two-wheelers up to and including 11 kW, from age 18 (Article 59). Class A covers two-wheelers above 125cc or above 11 kW, plus everything A1 covers. Under Article 89, an A1 licence issued before 1 January 2025 keeps its older scope, which is petrol two-wheelers from 50cc up to but not including 175cc.

That is the legal side. In practice, the A1 guide names the Honda Vision, Air Blade, Wave, Click and Yamaha Mio as models inside A1. The manufacturer pages give 110cc for the Vision, 109.2cc for the Wave Alpha 110, 110.3cc for the Sirius RC 110 and 125cc for the Air Blade 125, but Honda also lists an Air Blade 160, so ask the engine size of the bike you are offered, whatever its transmission. Most visitors do not hold a Vietnamese A1, and the A1 guide says recognition then depends on your home licence plus an International Driving Permit that includes a motorcycle category; [can foreigners rent a motorbike in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/can-foreigners-rent-a-motorbike-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) covers eligibility and documents.

## Mistakes that put renters on the wrong bike

- Taking a Wave or Sirius for a scooter. Both are easy to step onto, but each has a gear lever, so a rider who has never shifted should ask for an automatic.
- Grabbing the left lever by habit. On a scooter it is a brake; on a clutch bike it disconnects the engine, so a scooter rider who grabs it when startled slows nothing down and the bike keeps rolling. Close the throttle and use the brakes instead.
- Choosing a manual for control on a first Hanoi trip. The lever adds a task at every junction, which is the wrong trade while the traffic is new.
- Assuming one licence covers every engine size. The class follows engine size, so check the figure for the bike you are given.

## Common questions

### Is a semi-automatic the same as an automatic scooter?

No. A semi-automatic (xe so) has a gear lever for the left foot and no clutch lever; an automatic scooter (xe tay ga) has no gear lever at all. Neither has a hand clutch, which is why they are easy to confuse, so look at the left foot first.

### Which type suits a first-time renter in Hanoi?

An automatic. It leaves the throttle and the brakes to manage while the traffic is new, and four automatics are in the published rate list at 150,000 or 200,000 VND a day.

### Do I need a different licence for a manual-clutch bike?

The classes in the licence guides are set by engine size, electric power and number of wheels, and gearbox type is not one of the tests. What counts is the engine size of the bike (class A1 covers up to and including 125cc) and whether your home licence plus International Driving Permit is recognised.

### Can I learn to ride a clutch bike on a rental in Hanoi?

It can be done, but plan the practice before the rental. [The guide to learning manual on a rented bike]({{ '/articles/learning-manual-on-rented-bike-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) covers being upfront with the shop, looking after the clutch and practising off the street first.

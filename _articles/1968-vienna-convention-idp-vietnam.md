When people say "you need the right IDP for Vietnam", the "right" part comes from a treaty concluded in Vienna in 1968.

Short answer: Vietnam's road traffic law accepts a foreign international driving permit when a state that belongs to that treaty issued it and you carry it with the national licence it goes with, both suited to the bike (Article 57(6)(b) and (8)). Vietnam is a party itself: the United Nations records its accession on 20 August 2014.

## What the convention is

The Vienna Convention on Road Traffic (1968) is a United Nations treaty that sets mutual recognition rules for driving documents among its parties.

The [United Nations Treaty Collection][un-status] publishes the treaty's status record. On the page as it stood on 27 October 2026:

- The treaty is dated Vienna, 8 November 1968. A United Nations Conference on Road Traffic prepared it and opened it for signature at Vienna between 7 October and 8 November 1968, convened by the Secretary-General of the United Nations.
- It entered into force on 21 May 1977 under its article 47(1) and was registered the same day as No. 15705.
- The page counts 36 signatories and 91 parties.
- The certified text is in the United Nations Treaty Series, volume 1042, page 17. The page also lists notifications about later amendments, the latest dated 16 August 2024.

This guide quotes none of the treaty's own articles. What it says about the permit comes from the Vietnamese texts, because those are the ones a rider on a Vietnamese road is held to.

### Reading the status table

The table gives each participant a signature date and, in a second column, the date of accession, succession or ratification. Ninety-one rows carry a date in that second column, which matches the 91 parties the page counts. Ten rows show only a signature date: those states signed but have not ratified. A country with no row at all has done neither, and the United States, Canada and Malaysia have no row.

### Vietnam's entry

Vietnam acceded to the convention in 2014: its row shows accession on 20 August 2014. Its entry also records a notice choosing VN as the distinguishing sign for vehicles registered in Vietnam in international traffic, and one reservation, on article 52, the clause on settling disputes between parties.

## How Vietnamese law uses it

The convention by itself does not tell you what you may ride in Vietnam — domestic law does. Article 57(6)(b) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (No. 36/2024/QH15) recognises, on Vietnamese territory, [International Driving Permits]({{ '/articles/idp-vietnam-motorbike-rules/' | relative_url }}) and national licences issued by countries that are members of the 1968 convention, provided the licence matches the vehicle being ridden. Licences issued under the older 1949 Geneva Convention system are not in that list, which is why a 1949-convention IDP does not make motorbike riding legal here.

The wording used here is the consolidated text 55/VBHN-VPQH of March 2026, the Law as amended by Law 118/2025/QH15, which calls the treaty "the Vienna Convention". Article 57(6) lists four kinds of licence that are valid in Vietnam, and the convention is named in only one of them:

| Clause | What is valid in Vietnam | Where the convention comes in |
|---|---|---|
| 6(a) | A licence issued by a competent Vietnamese authority | Not at all |
| 6(b) | An international licence and a national licence, suited to the vehicle, from a member state | The clause that names the convention |
| 6(c) | A licence from a country or territory that has an agreement with Vietnam on mutual recognition | A separate route; no treaty named |
| 6(d) | A foreign licence, suited to the vehicle, from a state recognised under a treaty Vietnam has joined | A separate route; no treaty named |

Three things follow from how the clauses are built:

- **The test is the issuing state's membership.** Clause 6(b) turns on who issued the documents, so the status table comes before the design of the booklet.
- **Under clause 6(b) the permit does not stand alone.** Clause 6(b) names the international and the national licence together, and clause 8(b) makes the holder carry both, each suited to the class of vehicle and still in term. The Law never calls the permit a translation; the legal point is the pair.
- **The permit has a legal definition.** Clause 8(a) describes it as issued by the competent state authority of a member state in a uniform form, for a limited term tied to the national licence. [How long an IDP lasts]({{ '/articles/idp-validity-period-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) covers the dates.

## 1949 and 1968: why the year on the permit matters

Two treaties on the same subject exist; the UN Treaty Collection keeps the earlier one as a separate entry, chapter XI.B-1. Article 57 never mentions the 1949 Geneva Convention: clauses 6(b) and 8(a) name only the Vienna Convention. The safe reading for a rider is that a permit issued only under the 1949 system is not the document those clauses describe, although the Law has no sentence refusing 1949 permits and clause 6(d) names no treaty. A state's row in the table shows membership, not which treaty a booklet was issued under; the booklet's own text shows that.

## The category on the permit

Clauses 6(b) and 8(b) both ask for documents suited to the vehicle, but neither the Law nor Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA says which letters or words on a foreign permit stand for a motorbike. Appendix XII of the Circular, which Article 28 refers to, converts the classes of Vietnamese licences issued before 1 January 2025 into the classes of the permit Vietnam issues; it is not a table for foreign permits. In our reading the nearest yardstick is Article 57(1), which puts a two-wheeler up to 125 cm3, or an electric one up to 11 kW, in class A1 and a larger one in class A; [what the A1 class covers]({{ '/articles/a1-licence-vietnam-what-you-can-ride/' | relative_url }}) has the detail. A national licence for cars only leaves you at cars, whatever the permit says.

## Where the convention sits next to the exchange rule

Article 18(7) of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA sets the starting rule for foreigners and for Vietnamese residents abroad who need to drive in Vietnam. If such a person holds a national licence, point (a) sends them to the procedure for exchanging it for the corresponding Vietnamese licence. Point (b) lets an international treaty on driving licences to which Vietnam is a party provide otherwise, and then the treaty applies instead.

The 1968 Convention is a treaty to which Vietnam is a party, and Article 57(6)(b) of the Law gives its permits effect on Vietnamese roads. Our reading is that a visitor riding on a permit relies on that treaty exception, and that exchange is the starting rule for everyone else who needs to drive here.

Neither point of Article 18(7) fixes a date by which a resident on a permit must switch to exchange. For a stay of weeks or months, confirm in writing with the traffic police which route applies to you. [The foreign licence exchange guide]({{ '/articles/foreign-licence-exchange-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) covers who qualifies and the dossier, and [the expat licence guide]({{ '/articles/expat-motorbike-licence-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) weighs the options for a long stay.

## IDPs that Vietnam issues to its own licence holders

Vietnam issues permits as well as receiving them. The Law leaves the form, the issue and the use of the permit to the Minister of Public Security (Article 57(9), as amended), and Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, in force from 1 July 2026, sets them out in Articles 27 to 30.

- **Form (Article 27).** An A6 booklet, 148 mm by 105 mm, with security marks and a grey cover. The driver's details and class appear in Vietnamese, English, Russian, Spanish and French. The number is VN, Vietnam's distinguishing sign for international traffic, followed by the 12 digits of the national licence number; the UN status page records Vietnam's notice of the same sign.
- **Classes (Article 28).** They follow the classes of the Vietnamese national licence, as laid out in Appendix XII for licences issued before 1 January 2025.
- **Who can apply (Article 29).** Holders of a valid Vietnamese licence, and Vietnamese citizens or foreigners with a permanent or temporary residence card whose valid licence came from a state party to a treaty Vietnam has joined. No permit is issued for a licence that is erased, shows a difference in identification or was not issued by a competent authority.

Article 30(1) then closes the loop: a permit that Vietnam issues under the 1968 Convention has no validity for use within Vietnam. Whether another country accepts it is for that country's rules. Article 30(2) adds that a permit recognised under a treaty Vietnam has joined is handled under that treaty.

## How to check your permit

1. Look at your IDP booklet or card for a reference to the 1968 Vienna Convention
2. Check that a motorcycle category is shown on it
3. Check that your national licence also covers motorcycles
4. Carry both documents when riding — Vietnamese decree fines even valid IDP holders who leave the national licence at home

If your country does not issue 1968-convention IDPs, your route is either a Vietnamese licence or exchange of your national licence after establishing residence.

That is one choice for a rider whose country is outside the convention, not the only one. Article 57(6)(c) and (d) cover licences recognised under an agreement or a treaty, which an official source must confirm for your country, and the moped class needs no licence at all. Riders from [non-convention countries]({{ '/articles/non-1968-licence-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) have the options laid out.

### A longer check before you fly

1. **Find the issuing country in the status table** on the [UN Treaty Collection page][un-status]. A date in the last column means a party; a signature-only row does not.
2. **Read the booklet for the treaty it names,** and confirm that your own government's website lists the body that issued it.
3. **If any answer is uncertain, ask the issuing authority in writing** and keep the reply with your documents. [What a rider must carry]({{ '/articles/documents-to-carry-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) lists the other papers.

## Why this matters more than the fine

The fine for riding without valid documents is unpleasant; the accident scenario is worse. After a crash, an invalid licence can invalidate insurance claims and complicate liability, even if the other driver was at fault. Getting the paperwork right before you ride is the cheapest insurance you will ever arrange.

That last line is a figure of speech: a permit is a licence document, not a policy. Whether a licence problem defeats a claim depends on the policy wording and the facts of the crash, so ask your insurer in writing; the [travel insurance guide]({{ '/articles/motorbike-travel-insurance-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) has more on policy wording. Nguyen Tu does not provide motorbike insurance, so customers arrange their own. For the amounts, see [the no-licence fine guide]({{ '/articles/no-licence-fine-vietnam/' | relative_url }}); the decree behind them was amended in 2026.

## Questions riders ask

### Can I drive in Vietnam with an American licence?

Article 57(6)(b) turns on the issuing state being a member of the 1968 Convention, and the UN status table has no row for the United States. Nothing in Article 57 names the United States, so unless a treaty route under clause 6(c) or (d) applies, which only an official source can confirm, a US licence and permit are not the pair that clause describes. The routes left are the moped class, which needs no licence from age 16 ([what counts as a moped]({{ '/articles/what-is-a-moped-vietnam-law/' | relative_url }})), the exchange for residents and [the Vietnamese licence test]({{ '/articles/vietnam-licence-test-in-english/' | relative_url }}).

### Can I ride on a permit that Vietnam issued me?

No. Article 30(1) of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA says a permit issued by Vietnam under the 1968 Convention has no validity for use within Vietnam. Carry the Vietnamese licence instead.

### Where can I read the treaty itself?

The citation for the certified text is on the UN Treaty Collection page, in the record described above. Read the certified text rather than a summary before relying on any detail of the treaty's own articles.

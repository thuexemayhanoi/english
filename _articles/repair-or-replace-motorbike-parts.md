Every broken part eventually asks the same question: fix it, or fit a new one? The answer differs by part, by bike value and by safety role — and getting it wrong in either direction costs real money. Over-replacing burns budgets; over-repairing breaks bikes.

## The three categories

**Safety-critical parts: replace on schedule, repair rarely.** Brake pads, brake lines, steering bearings, tyres: these carry your life. The decision rule is simple — when a safety part is at its wear limit or damaged, it is replaced, and "repaired" applies only to cleaning and adjustment. A braided patch on a brake line is not a thing. If a mechanic offers a clever repair on a safety part, that is the mechanic you replace instead.

**Wear parts: replace when the math says so.** Chains, sprockets, plugs, filters, cables, batteries. These have service lives, and the decision is mostly timing. Two honest rules: replace wear parts in matched groups where the manual says so (chain and sprockets together; pads in pairs — see chain-sprocket-replacement-motorbike); and replace a wear part *before* it damages its expensive neighbour (a worn chain eats sprockets; a worn pad eats a disc).

**Everything else: repair is often the smart money.** Bodywork, brackets, stands, levers, seats: in Vietnam, repair culture is a genuine economic advantage — welding, straightening, re-covering and re-threading are cheap and skilled here, and a straightened bracket is often as good as new for a fraction of the price. Ask a mechanic what they would do on their own bike — the phrasing is everything, and the technique is in talking-to-mechanic-vietnam.

## The three questions that decide close calls

1. **What does the quote say?** A repair that costs a large fraction of a new part, on a part that will need buying soon anyway, is not a repair — it is an instalment plan. Get the quote for both routes; comparing quotes is covered in repair-quotes-parts-pricing-vietnam.
2. **What is the bike worth?** On a bike worth a few million dong, a repair at 30% of replacement cost is sensible; on a newer bike, the calculation tilts toward the new part and its warranty. The economics of keeping an older bike alive are in keeping-old-motorbike-alive-high-mileage.
3. **How available is the part?** A cheap new part that takes three weeks to arrive versus a competent repair today is not always a win for waiting — the availability map is in spare-parts-availability-vietnam.

## The used-part exception

Used parts flip the math on expensive items: a used, undamaged component from a breaker can be the honest middle path — with the discipline of inspecting it as carefully as the part it replaces (buying guidance is in buying-motorbike-parts-online-vietnam). The rule: used parts for everything except safety-critical and hidden-failure items.

## A final honest note

The repair-or-replace question is best asked *before* the failure, at service time: parts near their limits are cheapest to plan, and a planned decision is always cheaper than one made roadside. Mechanics who know you will usually lay out both routes honestly when asked directly — the habit of asking is the whole skill.

Nhat Tan is Hanoi's peach-blossom village: the riverside gardens where much of the city's Tet peach — the pink blossom that defines Lunar New Year in the north — is grown. For a few weeks each winter the village turns into a staging area of blossoming trees, buyers' trucks and photo-hunting families, and a motorbike is the only sensible way to visit it.

## When to go

The season runs with the Tet calendar, not the solar one. The blossom arrives with the winter monsoon chill — the gardens begin to colour as Hanoi gets cold, in the last lunar month before Tet — and peaks in the days immediately before the festival, when the city buys its trees. After Tet 
t
he show is over for the year. Outside the blossom weeks, Nhat Tan is still a working flower village of chrysanthemums and garden flowers, but the peach spectacle is seasonal: check where Tet falls in your travel year, and aim for the two weeks before it.

## The route

The village sits on the Red River bank beside the northeast of West Lake, reached by the dyke road — the same dyke system as the flower-market ride, continued north. From central Hanoi, ride to West Lake, follow the lake's northern shore, and pick up the Nghi Tam–Tu Lien dyke line toward Nhat Tan. The dyke section is one of the city's favourite easy rides, and in peach season it becomes a slow parade of motorbikes carrying wrapped trees.

In season, expect congestion: the garden lanes fill with families photographing each other, and the through-flow drops to walking pace at the good spots. That is the correct pace for this ride anyway.

## On arrival

The gardens are commercial: you are photographing someone's crop. Most garden owners welcome visitors and charge a small entry or photo fee in the peak weeks — pay it without discussion; it is how the village shares the season's income. Never break branches or pull blossoms, and keep to the paths between the beds. There is attended parking at the garden lane entrances.

## Riding home with a tree

Every year, thousands of peach trees ride home on motorbikes — wrapped in paper and plastic, balanced on the rack or carried across the rider's arms, an image that is itself Hanoi. If you buy one for an apartment or hotel, have the garden wrap it tightly, strap it with the trunk as the spine, and ride slowly: a top-heavy tree changes the bike's balance more than any luggage. The cargo guide covers the strapping principles; blossom branches define the width of your load.

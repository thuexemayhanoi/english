A chain that jumps, clatters or demands adjustment every week is near the end of its life. Knowing the difference between a chain that needs lubrication, one that needs adjustment, and one that is finished saves both money and the very real risk of a chain snapping mid-ride.

## The signs of a finished chain

Rust that does not wipe off, stiff or seized links that kink instead of bending smoothly, and adjustment at its limit — the rear wheel sits as far back as it can and the chain still has visible slack. By hand: pull the chain away from the middle of the rear sprocket. If it lifts far enough to see daylight under it, or the rollers are worn to hook shapes, replacement time has arrived. chain-maintenance-motorbike-vietnam covers everything before this point.

## Why sprockets go with the chain

A worn chain wears the sprockets to its own stretched pitch; putting a new chain on worn, hooked sprocket teeth wears out the new chain early and can make it jump under load. Shops replace the set together for that reason — replacing one and not the other wastes the new part.

## Teeth tell the story

Healthy sprocket teeth look symmetrical, like a well-kept saw blade. Worn teeth look like ocean waves or hooks, leaning in the direction of drive. On O-ring chains, respect the O-rings: no wire-brushing or aggressive solvents — the O-rings hold the factory lubricant inside the rollers, and destroying them turns a durable chain into a short-lived one.

## The work itself

Replacement needs the right tools, correct tension and alignment after fitting — both wheels must run true or the new chain wears crooked.

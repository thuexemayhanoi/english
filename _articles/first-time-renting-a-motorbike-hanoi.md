If you have never rented a motorbike anywhere, Hanoi is an intense place to start. The process is simple; the traffic is the exam. Renting a motorbike in Hanoi for the first time works when you hold a valid licence, take an automatic and keep day one small; it goes wrong when your first ride in traffic is also your first lesson. Here is the walkthrough in the order it happens to you.

## Decide first: are you ready to rent?

Readiness is your call, and it is easier to make at home than next to a bike. Find the line that sounds like you.

| If you are... | Do this |
|---|---|
| New to riding altogether | Learn somewhere quiet before you rent here, or see the city as a passenger for now |
| A licensed scooter or moped rider at home | Rent an automatic and follow the first-hour plan below |
| A manual or large-bike rider at home | Your balance and braking carry over; reading Hanoi traffic does not, so start on an automatic anyway |
| Not sure your licence counts here | Settle it before you book, because it decides whether you may ride a bike of this size at all |

If the first row sounds like you, [the complete-beginner guide]({{ '/articles/renting-motorbike-hanoi-complete-beginner/' | relative_url }}) sets out a learning path. A licence-free class such as a 50 cc bike or an e-bike removes the licence problem, not the skill problem, so the first row still applies to it. Waiting is a legitimate choice: taxis and ride-hailing cars cover the trips that matter, and a rental made once the first row no longer describes you is likelier to go well.

## Before you go: the licence check

Do not skip this. To ride legally you need a licence Vietnam recognises - for example, a home motorcycle licence together with a 1968-convention IDP. If you hold no recognised licence, your legal options are a 50 cc bike or a licence-free e-bike. The licence articles cover the routes; decide before you shop, not at the counter.

Article 57 of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety sets out which licences count; a 1949 Geneva permit or a permit without a motorcycle category does not, and the [tourist licence guide]({{ '/articles/can-tourists-ride-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) gives the three routes.

As practical advice, separate from the law: check your permit's categories before you fly, and keep the permit and your licence together whenever you ride.

## Choosing your first rental bike

Automatic scooter, full stop. No gears to manage while you learn how this city moves. The common automatic rentals (Honda Vision, Honda Click, Yamaha Mio) are light, low-geared for city speeds, and forgiving. Semiautomatic underbones like the Honda Wave are the local workhorse and are cheap, but the foot-shift adds one thing to think about on day one. Save manuals for later.

At the published day rates a first day costs 150,000 VND on a Honda Click or Yamaha Mio and 200,000 VND on a Honda Vision, before the deposit. ## At the shop

Passport, deposit (typically 2,000,000-5,000,000 VND), rental price - cash, transfer or QR. Run the [inspection checklist]({{ '/articles/rental-motorbike-inspection-checklist/' | relative_url }}) even though everything is new to you: lights, horn, brakes, mirrors, tyres, fuel. Photograph existing scratches. Ask the one beginner question that matters: how does the side stand / centre stand / steering lock work on this bike.

The transaction itself has its own guides: [how the whole rental runs]({{ '/articles/how-to-rent-a-motorbike-hanoi/' | relative_url }}), [which documents the counter asks for]({{ '/articles/documents-needed-rent-motorbike-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) and [how to book by WhatsApp, Zalo or walk-in]({{ '/articles/how-to-book-a-motorbike-rental-hanoi/' | relative_url }}). What a first-timer adds is a short list of requests:

- Say it is your first rental, and ask to be shown the controls you need in the first minute: starting the bike, the stands, the steering lock, the indicators and the horn.
- Ask whether a helmet comes with the bike. If one does, check it before you leave: an undamaged shell, a working strap and a snug fit; if it fails, ask whether another is available, or bring your own.
- Ask who pays for a puncture. Consumables such as inner tubes and tyres may be the renter's responsibility depending on the situation.
- Save the shop's number, +84 942 467 674 (phone, WhatsApp or Zalo), so that a problem on the road starts with a call rather than a search.

## The first ten minutes

Ride the block around the shop before committing to the road. Practise in this order: throttle control (a typical automatic does not creep at idle, but a small turn of the throttle moves it off and a sharp twist sends it forward quickly - keep a brake covered), both brakes (each lever on its own, then both together, squeezing progressively rather than grabbing; ask which wheel each lever works, as some scooters link the brakes), and mirrors. Then enter traffic with three habits: slow, predictable lines; horn before you drift past anything; and never assume a bus has seen you.

### A plan for the first hour

Ten minutes is the minimum. With an hour, spend it like this, and leave the block only when each step feels dull:

1. **Set up while parked.** Dress for a fall (closed shoes, long trousers), set both mirrors, find the horn, indicators and brake levers by feel, and practise putting each foot down.
2. **Walk it, then roll it.** Paddle the bike a few metres to feel its weight, then roll at walking pace and stop with both brakes.
3. **Slow-speed drills.** Walking-pace straight lines, gentle figures of eight and one wide U-turn, eyes on where you want to go; [the slow-speed control guide]({{ '/articles/slow-speed-control-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) has the drills.
4. **Shoulder checks.** Indicate, check the mirror and glance over your shoulder in a straight line; if the handlebars move, repeat step 3.
5. **One quiet street and back.** Ride it at a steady pace and return the same way before trying a loop.

If a step will not settle after a few repeats, treat that as information, not failure; the signals for handing the bike back are further down. Braking, junctions, rain and night riding are covered by [the riding skills and safety guides]({{ '/topics/safety/' | relative_url }}); this page stays on readiness.

## Your first day: a quiet route at a quiet time

### Choose the route before you collect the bike

Choose it at a desk, not at the first junction. For a first ride, look for:

- a wide road with clear lane markings and a steady flow, or a quiet residential street with little through traffic, rather than a narrow lane where everyone weaves;
- few junctions and no large roundabouts;
- a loop made of right turns: Vietnam drives on the right, so a right turn does not cross oncoming traffic, while a left turn across a stream is a skill for later;
- somewhere to pull over, turn round and park.

Leave the Old Quarter's narrow streets, bridges and any junction fed by a continuous stream for later. The shop is in Long Bien, on the east side of the Red River, so the way to a central hotel crosses a bridge: plan that crossing as its own daylight ride, and check the first turn out of the shop on a map beforehand so you know it before you start the engine.

### Choose the time

Daylight, dry roads and thin traffic matter more than the day of the week. The shop opens at 09:00, so a morning handover gives a first ride the most light and leaves the afternoon for a calm return. Start after the commuter rush rather than inside it, and respect the evening peak on the way back. In the rainy season, check the sky before you leave and treat a wet road as a reason to wait.

### Plan the way back

Decide the return before you leave: a road you already know, in daylight, with margin. Late return is charged at 20,000 VND per hour, so build in time rather than racing the clock. If you land in Hanoi the same day, rest first; [the airport arrival guide]({{ '/articles/renting-motorbike-noi-bai-arrival/' | relative_url }}) covers the first day.

## Rules that keep first-timers legal

Keep right, no [sidewalk riding]({{ '/articles/riding-on-sidewalk-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) (fined), no phone in hand (fined), helmet on. Traffic moves by negotiation - read the [lane-rules article]({{ '/articles/motorbike-lane-rules-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) before your first big junction.

What those rules mean in practice, as the legal guides on this site set them out:

- **Helmet:** fastened, for rider and passenger; an unstrapped helmet counts as none ([Vietnam's helmet law]({{ '/articles/helmet-law-vietnam/' | relative_url }})).
- **Lane:** the lane for your direction, the right-hand part of the carriageway where there are no markings, never across a fixed median.
- **Sidewalk:** pedestrians only, apart from crossing to enter or leave premises and brief low-speed manoeuvres where there is no alternative. If a street is blocked, get off and walk the bike.
- **Phone:** holding one while riding is an offence, navigation included, while a proper mount used without your hands leaving the controls is different ([the phone-use rules]({{ '/articles/phone-use-while-riding-vietnam/' | relative_url }})). Set the destination before you move.
- **Alcohol:** any detectable amount on a motorbike is an offence ([the drink-driving rules]({{ '/articles/drink-driving-limits-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }})).

## The first week: mistakes and what to do instead

Five common first-week mistakes, why each hurts, and what to do instead.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Do this instead |
|---|---|---|
| Raising the difficulty on day two | Confidence outruns skill, and traffic is where the gap shows | Change one thing at a time, the route or the hour |
| Copying locals who thread through gaps | The lane rules police gap-riding, and copying it makes you unpredictable | Hold a steady line and leave space |
| Checking the map in one hand | Handheld phone use is an offence | Mount the phone and set the destination first |
| Taking a passenger early | A pillion upsets slow-speed balance | Ride alone until the controls feel automatic |
| Riding tired or after a drink | Fatigue breeds sloppy lane discipline, and any alcohol is an offence | Stop and rest, or take a taxi |

## What nobody tells beginners

Everyone in Hanoi traffic is predicting everyone else; being predictable is a safety feature. Slow is legal, slow is fine. And the shop is a resource - if the bike feels wrong on day one, take it back; that is what the maintenance side of the rental is for.

## When to stop and hand the bike back

"Take it back" needs one qualification, and it is not the only reason to stop. Four situations call for it, each with a different next step.

**The bike.** Brakes that do not bite, steering that fights you, a new wobble, or a fuel smell with a visible leak: park, switch off and call the shop rather than riding the bike back. [What to do if your rental does not feel normal]({{ '/articles/rental-motorbike-feels-wrong/' | relative_url }}) lists the stop-now and report-soon symptoms. Routine maintenance such as chain adjustment and tyre-pressure checks may be part of what the business handles, but no rescue service or response time is published and support is aimed at local use in Hanoi. Keep the first days inside the city and keep your phone charged.

**You.** Shaking hands, repeated near misses, tunnel vision at junctions, hunger, heat or fading light all mean the same thing: stop somewhere safe. A taxi or ride-hailing car is a normal way home. An attended motorbike lot is a sensible place to leave the bike until you are rested; tell the shop where the bike is.

**A fall.** If a fall hurts anyone, damages property or involves a collision with another person or vehicle, treat it as a road accident; the duties do not shrink with the speed: stop, warn of danger, preserve the scene, help anyone injured and notify the authorities, calling 113 for the police and 115 if anyone is hurt. [What to do after a motorbike accident in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/motorbike-accident-duties-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) sets them out. As practical additions, keep the shop's number to hand and photograph the positions and damage before anything is moved, if it is safe to do so.

**The rental itself.** If you decide riding is not for you, tell the shop promptly. Early return carries no automatic refund for unused time, which is the main reason to start with a short rental rather than a long one.

## Common questions

### Can I rent a motorbike in Hanoi with no riding experience?

Renting needs documents and a deposit, riding legally needs a valid licence, and neither measures skill, which is why the readiness check comes first. Treat a lesson or a test ride as something to ask about, not something included. If you have never ridden, the readiness check at the top of this page is the place to start.

### Does the rental include insurance?

No. Nguyen Tu does not provide motorbike insurance, so renters arrange their own. Check what your own policy says about riding a motorbike and which licence it expects: an invalid licence can undermine an insurance claim after a crash.

### Is Hanoi traffic too much for a first-timer?

It is demanding rather than impossible. It rewards a steady, predictable pace, a fastened helmet and a start outside rush hour, and it punishes the opposite. The first-hour plan and route advice above show how to build that pace.

### How long should my first rental be?

Short. Rentals run by the day, week or month, with longer terms negotiable, and a day or two is a reasonable first test. The section on handing the bike back explains why: early return carries no automatic refund.

## The short version

First-rental nerves are normal. Let them keep your speed down; if they are not easing as the controls become familiar, the hand-back section above applies. Rent the automatic, do the checklist, ride the block, then the city.

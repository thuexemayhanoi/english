Short answer: a moped needs no driving licence from age 16, but anything bigger needs a licence that Vietnam recognises for that class of bike. Riding a bigger bike without one is an administrative fine, it leaves you in a weak position after an accident, and no rental paperwork changes that. Here is what actually happens to unlicensed riders.

## What you may ride with no licence at all

The only licence-free motorbike class is the moped. Article 34(1)(g) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (No. 36/2024/QH15, as amended by Law 118/2025/QH15) defines a moped as a two- or three-wheeled engine-driven vehicle designed for no more than 50 km/h. A combustion engine must not exceed 50 cm3, and an electric motor must not exceed 4 kW. The definition leaves out the motor-assisted bicycle, a bicycle whose motor cuts out when you stop pedalling or when the bike reaches 25 km/h, which the Law lists separately among non-motorised vehicles (Article 34(2)(b)).

Two conditions come with the class. Article 59(1)(a) sets the minimum age at 16, and Article 56(4) requires a moped rider to know the road rules, to have the skills to control the vehicle and to meet the age and health conditions. Article 56(1), which demands a valid licence from every other driver, carves moped riders out. The [50cc licence guide]({{ '/articles/50cc-licence-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) walks through the class in more detail, and the page on [foreign riders on a 50cc]({{ '/articles/can-foreigners-ride-50cc-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) confirms that the moped rule applies to foreigners too.

What the class does not give you is permission to stop checking. A rental listing that says "50 cc" is not the legal test; the definition is. A bike built for a higher speed than 50 km/h is a motorcycle under Article 34(1)(e), which covers every two- or three-wheeled engine-driven vehicle that is not a moped, and the licence rules of the next section then apply to it. An electric scooter with a motor above 4 kW is in the same position.

Ask the shop for the design speed and the engine or motor figure, and choose another bike if nobody can give them. The class lifts the licence requirement and nothing else: every other traffic rule still applies, and so do the consequences of an accident described below.

## What a licence must cover above that class

Above the moped class the licence has to pass four tests at once. Article 56(1) requires it to be valid, to still carry points, to fit the vehicle being ridden and to have been issued by a competent authority. Article 57(1) then sets the motorbike classes: A1 for a two-wheeled motorbike of up to 125 cm3 or an electric motor of up to 11 kW, and class A above either limit, which also covers everything A1 covers. The car classes are listed separately in the same article, so a licence that holds only car classes does not reach a motorbike.

Which documents count? Article 57(6) accepts four: a licence issued by a Vietnamese authority; an IDP together with a national licence, both issued by a state party to the 1968 Vienna Convention and suited to the vehicle; and two treaty routes, one for countries or territories that have a mutual-recognition agreement with Vietnam and one for countries recognised under a treaty that Vietnam has joined. The table further down matches each document to what you hold.

An IDP does not stand alone. Article 57(8)(a) says it is issued in the uniform Vienna Convention format, runs for no more than three years from issue and must fit the remaining validity of the national licence. Article 57(8)(b) requires a rider on an IDP to carry both documents, each suited to the class of bike and still in date, and Article 57(7)(a) makes an expired licence invalid. A permit issued by Vietnam is no workaround: Articles 27 to 30 of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA deal with the IDP, and Article 30(1) says an IDP issued by Vietnam under the 1968 Convention has no validity inside Vietnam.

If you live in Vietnam, the foreign licence can become a Vietnamese one. Article 24(1)(a) of the same Circular, in force from 1 July 2026, lets a foreigner who lives, works or studies in Vietnam and holds a valid national licence apply for the matching Vietnamese class. The applicant also needs a visa or temporary residence card valid for 90 days or more, a permanent residence card, or a diplomatic or service identity card. Article 24(5)(a) ties the term of the Vietnamese licence to one of those documents and to the foreign licence, so an exchange made on a short visa gives a short licence.

Article 24(1)(c) rules out exchanging an IDP or an expired foreign licence. Someone with no motorbike licence anywhere has nothing to exchange; a Vietnamese licence then comes through a test, and Article 59(1)(b) sets the minimum age for classes A1, A and B1 at 18.

## What the law does

The licence duty sits with the person who rides: Article 56(1) speaks of the driver taking part in road traffic, and a rental contract does not appear in it. Riding without a valid licence is an administrative fine under Decree 168/2024/NĐ-CP, which Decree 238/2026/NĐ-CP amends from 15 August 2026. The amount depends on the bike and on the text of the decree in force on the day of the violation, so see the fine guide linked in the last section for the figures.

Alongside the fine, traffic police can hold the vehicle until the situation is resolved - and a rider who cannot produce any papers at all can have the bike held until papers appear. A fine is the good outcome. Which cases allow holding is set in the same fines decree, so check the current list in the police impound guide linked in the last section before you rely on it.

## What an accident does

The fine is the smallest risk. Article 83(1)(b) of the Law says that a person who causes a road accident may, depending on the nature and degree of the violation, be dealt with administratively or prosecuted criminally, and must compensate any damage in accordance with the law. The article applies to whoever causes the accident, licensed or not, but for an unlicensed rider the missing licence becomes part of the facts of the case.

For an accident that does not yet show signs of a crime, Article 83(2) lists what the investigation covers, including checking the conditions of the vehicle and of the driver with the related papers, and temporarily holding vehicles, documents and licences. Article 80(1) also requires a rider who causes a road accident to stop at once, warn of the danger, keep the scene as it is, help the injured, report to the nearest police, medical facility or People's Committee, and stay until the police arrive. The exceptions are going for emergency care, taking the injured for emergency care, or a threat to the rider's life or health, and even then the rider must report at once to the nearest police or People's Committee.

The other party's insurers or lawyers will ask for your licence. That applies once a claim is made against you; what a particular insurer or lawyer asks for is for them to say. How much is owed, and by whom, depends on the facts, on any insurance in play and on the contract.

The shop renting to you is not a defence; the law puts the duty on the rider, and the shop faces its own penalty for handing a bike to an unqualified rider. The owner's separate penalty for letting an unqualified person ride is set out in the guide on [lending a motorbike to an unlicensed rider]({{ '/articles/lending-motorbike-unlicensed-rider-vietnam/' | relative_url }}).

## What your travel insurance does

Travel policies that cover motorcycling can carry a licence condition. Wording differs from policy to policy, so read for the licence condition, any engine-size limit and any list of excluded activities. Australia's official travel advice for Vietnam says that authorities may fine you for driving without a valid licence, and adds: "Your travel insurer will likely deny any claims you make if you're unlicensed or you don't hold the correct class of licence."

Australia's advice, like the UK government's, is to check that your policy covers your planned activity. "Likely" is the government's word: whether one insurer pays an unlicensed rider is for that insurer to say, in writing, on the wording of your policy.

Three different things get called insurance around a rental, and mixing them up leaves gaps:

- The vehicle owner's compulsory civil-liability insurance. Article 56(1)(d) lists its certificate among the papers a driver carries. It belongs to the vehicle and its owner, not to you as a rider, and the [compulsory insurance guide]({{ '/articles/compulsory-motorbike-insurance-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) sets out what it pays for.
- Your own travel or medical policy. You arrange it, and you read its exclusions.
- Cover from the rental business. Nguyen Tu does not provide motorbike insurance; renters arrange their own cover. With any other business, ask what cover is offered and get the answer in writing.

Read the exclusion wording before you ride, not after. Then put one question to your insurer in writing: does the licence I hold count as valid for the bike I plan to rent in Vietnam? Keep the reply with your booking. The [travel insurance fine-print guide]({{ '/articles/motorbike-travel-insurance-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) lists the clauses worth checking.

## Questions to ask yourself before you book

1. Does my licence include a motorbike category that reaches the bike I want? A1 stops at 125 cc, or 11 kW for an electric motor; anything bigger needs class A (Article 57(1)).
2. Is the bike genuinely in the moped class?
3. Has my insurer told me in writing that I am covered on this bike with this licence?
4. How will the registration certificate and the compulsory insurance certificate be shown if I am stopped? Ask at handover; the guide on [what a rider must carry]({{ '/articles/documents-to-carry-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) has the full list.

If any answer is no, use the table above to pick a legal fallback rather than an informal workaround.

### Does a short ride or a quiet road change the rule?

Article 56(1) applies to a driver taking part in road traffic, and the only exception it names is the moped rider covered by clause 4. It says nothing about the length of the ride.

## The bottom line

Renting is a contract; the licence rules govern riding. A shop's willingness to hand over a bike is not a legal clearance for you, and the gap can come to light at a police stop, after an accident or when you claim on insurance. If your paperwork does not cover a motorcycle, ride a 50 cc bike, ride an e-bike, or ride nothing.

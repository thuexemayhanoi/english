Old Hanoi is organised by trade: one street sells silk, another bamboo, another tin boxes. Riders benefit from the same logic — the city has streets and clusters where the motorbike trade concentrates, where a repair, a part or a piece of gear can be found faster than in any other city in the region. Knowing where these places are turns a breakdown from a crisis into an errand.

## The repair clusters

Kerbside repair happens everywhere — a man with a compressor under a tarp can fix a flat in ten minutes on almost any arterial street, and the flat-tyre guide covers that world. But for real problems, the trade clusters:

- **Specialist repair streets** — several streets in the inner wards (the area around Hang Cot / the northern Old Quarter fringe is the historic bike-trade street; similar clusters run through Hai Ba Trung and Dong Da) host shops with lifts, diagnostic habits and staff who rebuild engines on benches. A walk down one of these streets teaches you more about the fleet than any article: every shop specializes, and they signal it with the bikes parked in front.
- **Tyre-and-battery shops** cluster at main intersections in every district; punctures and battery replacements are the most frequent repairs in the city and never far away.
- **Electric-bike shops** are a fast-growing separate trade — relevant if you ride an e-bike, since the repair conventions (battery diagnostics, controller swaps) are their own world.

## How the kerbside economy works

No appointment, no written estimate, sometimes no common language. The mechanic looks, pokes, names a part, quotes a number. For small jobs it is efficient and honest; for big jobs the advice in the talking-to-a-mechanic guide applies — get the quote before the work, watch the part go in, keep the old part. Quality varies enormously between shops with identical street setups; a shop that is busy with local residents is usually a shop that locals trust.

## Parts

The parts trade has its own geography: physical markets for new and used parts (the big multi-story parts market in the north of the city is the classic source; the parts-buying guide covers it), plus a thriving online trade. The genuine-versus-fake question is the eternal one — Vietnam's fleet runs on a mix of OEM, OEM-copy and "good enough" aftermarket, and the price gap is your main signal. For anything safety-related (brake pads, cables, tyres), buy the better option.

## Gear

Helmet shops cluster near the tourist quarters and around the big dealerships: the dedicated gear shops sell certified full-face and flip-up helmets, gloves and rain gear, while the street vendors near the bridge approaches sell the ten-dollar shells that satisfy the letter of the law and nothing else. The helmet standards guide explains the difference in full; for gear shopping, the rule of thumb is that a proper gear shop lets you try things on and has sizing ranges.

## For rental riders

If your rental bike develops a problem, the first call is the rental shop, not the nearest street — most problems on rental bikes are minor and the shop may send someone or tell you exactly which shop to use. Unauthorized major work on a rental is a contract question before it is a mechanical one; the outside-repair guide covers what renters may and may not do.

Written as streets and clusters, the system sounds chaotic. Ridden as a system, it is one of Hanoi's genuine comforts: whatever breaks, the answer is within twenty minutes, and someone there has fixed it ten thousand times.

Brakes announce their decline politely: a lever that travels further, a squeal, a shudder. Riders who check catch the polite phase; riders who do not meet the abrupt one. This article covers the checks. Pad thickness limits, fluid types and bleed intervals are model-specific — the owner's manual or a qualified mechanic is the authority for those.

## Feel first

Pull the front lever: it should be firm within a short, consistent travel, with no sponginess — spongy means air or moisture in the system and needs workshop attention. Press the rear pedal: same test. Roll the bike and apply each brake separately: the bike should stop squarely without pulling to one side, and without a grinding feel. The grinding feel is metal on metal — that is a park-it-now finding.

## Look at the pads

On most bikes you can see the pads through the caliper or behind the disc: pads wear visibly thinner over time, and when the friction material approaches the backing plate, they are due. On drum brakes (many Vietnamese underbones), the sign is a steadily increasing lever travel that adjustment no longer fixes. Any brake fluid leak visible around caliper or master cylinder is a workshop item immediately.

## Sounds and shudders

A light occasional squeal can be dust; a constant squeal, a gritty feel, or a pulsing lever under braking points to a glazed, warped or contaminated disc. A lever that pumps up firmer after a few pulls suggests air in the line. All of these are mechanic conversations this week, not next month.

## Riding and checking together

Braking technique (progressive squeeze, front-first) is covered in emergency-braking-motorbike-vietnam; the weekly two-minute habit that includes brakes is in motorbike-safety-checklist-before-riding. ## The one-line version

Firm levers, no sponginess, no grinding, pads visibly thicker than their plate — and any change from yesterday's feel is the bike asking for a mechanic.

Two people on one motorbike is a familiar sight in Vietnam, and renting as a couple follows the same pattern — with a few specifics worth knowing before the handover. Yes, a couple can ride one rented bike two-up: one licensed rider, one passenger and two fastened helmets. This guide covers the passenger rule, whether one bike or two suits you, what to check and ask at handover, and how to ride two-up comfortably.

## The law: one passenger, correctly seated

Vietnamese traffic law allows a two-wheel motorbike or moped to carry one passenger, and on a bike built for two that means one person on the seat behind the rider. Article 33(1) of Law 36/2024/QH15 allows up to two passengers only in four listed cases: taking a sick person for emergency care, escorting a person who has committed a violation, a child under 12, and a frail, elderly or disabled person. Outside those cases a second passenger is not allowed, and Article 33 also bars carrying a person who stands on the vehicle or the luggage rack or sits on the handlebars.

The full rules — including the narrow exceptions — are in [Carrying Passengers on a Motorbike in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/motorbike-passenger-rules-vietnam/' | relative_url }}).

### What the law asks of the passenger

Article 33(4) lists what a passenger must not do while the bike is on the road: carry bulky objects, use an umbrella, hold on to, pull or push other vehicles, stand on the seat or the luggage rack, or sit on the handlebars. For a couple the practical point is luggage. A passenger may not carry bulky objects, so decide before you ride what goes in the underseat box and what stays at your accommodation.

## One bike or two?

Renting for two people can mean one bike with a passenger or two bikes with a rider each. Four things decide it: who holds a licence valid for the bike, how confident each of you is on a motorbike, how far you plan to go, and what you want to spend.

| If this is you | Better fit | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Only one of you holds a licence valid for the bike | One bike, ridden by the licence holder | The other person rides as a passenger and never takes the controls |
| Both of you hold valid licences and want to ride independently | Two bikes | Each rider controls their own bike and neither depends on the other's pace |
| One of you is new to motorbikes | One bike, ridden by the experienced partner | A newcomer can learn the roads as a passenger before riding alone |
| A short city day with little luggage | One bike | One bike to check at handover and one to park |
| You want to split up for part of the trip | Two bikes | Neither of you is tied to the other's plans |

Cost is the other half of the choice. ## Choosing the bike

Two-up changes the maths: you want seat length for two, suspension that does not bottom out, and enough torque that the bike still pulls cleanly with a passenger.

The comparison of candidates, including which class of bike suits two people, is in [Best Motorbike for Two People in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/best-motorbike-for-two-people-hanoi/' | relative_url }}).

Agree with the shop what you plan; a bike that is fine solo can feel underpowered and harsh two-up on Hanoi's speed bumps.

### What to check on the bike itself

A short test at the counter tells you more than a spec sheet. Sit on the bike together, with the helmets on, and look at five things.

- Seat: both of you should fit without the passenger pushing the rider forward; a passenger sliding into the rider's back means the seat is too short or too sloped for two.
- Footpegs: the passenger's pegs should be present, firm and on both sides, since a passenger whose feet dangle is more likely to put a foot down at a stop.
- Something to hold: a grab rail or handle behind the passenger, or the rider's hips if the bike has none.
- Rear suspension: press down on the back of the seat; the bike should resist and rise back, not sink and stay down.
- Tyres and brakes: ask whether the tyre pressure has been checked for two-up riding, then roll forward a few metres with both of you aboard and brake gently.

Helmet storage matters at every stop. Honda's page for the Air Blade 125 lists a 23.2 L underseat box and describes it as fitting two half-helmets. No underseat figure is recorded for the Honda Vision, the Honda Wave Alpha 110 or the Yamaha Sirius RC 110, so check whether there is a box at all and what fits.

## Helmets: two, not one

Ask at handover whether helmets come with the bike and whether there are two in your sizes. Article 33(2) of Law 36/2024/QH15 requires the rider and every passenger to wear a helmet that meets the national technical standard, with the strap fastened correctly, and [the helmet law guide]({{ '/articles/helmet-law-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) sets out the standard and the fine.

A loose helmet on a pillion is close to no helmet; adjust or ask for a swap. Try each helmet on at the counter: it should sit level, the strap should fasten snugly under the chin, and the helmet should not rock when the wearer shakes their head. Check the passenger's helmet as carefully as your own. A novelty helmet without certification does not satisfy the standard, and a cracked shell or a failing strap is a reason to ask for another or bring your own.

See [Do Hanoi Rentals Include Helmets?]({{ '/articles/do-hanoi-rentals-include-helmets/' | relative_url }}) for the general policy picture.

## Deposit and terms

Nothing changes structurally for a couple — the deposit runs typically 2,000,000–5,000,000 VND depending on the bike ([Motorbike Rental Deposits in Hanoi: Typical Amounts and Return]({{ '/articles/motorbike-rental-deposit-hanoi/' | relative_url }})), and the agreement terms are the ones in [Understanding a Motorbike Rental Agreement in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/motorbike-rental-agreement-hanoi/' | relative_url }}). One detail worth deciding before rental: who is the named rider. The licence-holder must be the one riding; swapping controls mid-trip with an unlicensed partner is the classic way couples end up in the [The Fine for Riding Without a Licence in Vietnam]({{ '/articles/no-licence-fine-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) situation.

Even between two licence holders, Article 33(3) of the law bars changing the rider while the bike is moving, so swap places only after it has stopped. If the second person holds a licence valid for the bike, ask whether they can be named on the agreement as well.

Two more points belong in the conversation at the counter:

- Insurance: Nguyen Tu does not provide motorbike insurance, so arrange your own cover. If you rely on travel insurance, ask the insurer whether it covers riding a rented motorbike and carrying a passenger, and keep the answer in writing.
- Papers: ask at handover how the registration certificate and the compulsory insurance certificate will be shown at a stop.

## Technique for a comfortable pillion

- Rider: brake earlier and smoother than solo; pillions feel every jerk twice
- Pillion: hold the rail or the rider's hips, keep feet on the pegs, and lean with the bike, not against it
- Agree signals before setting out: a tap on the shoulder means slow down
- First ride together should be a calm street, not rush hour

### Getting on and moving off

The rider mounts first, settles, and holds the bike upright with both feet down. The passenger gets on only when the rider says so and puts both feet on the pegs before the bike moves. Balance at walking pace is where a passenger changes the bike most, so use a gentle throttle and ask the passenger to sit still until you are rolling.

### Braking, bumps and corners

Start braking earlier than you would alone and squeeze both brakes smoothly. There is more weight to stop, and a sudden stop throws the passenger into the rider's back. Slow right down for speed bumps and potholes, where the rear of the bike takes the weight of two people. In a corner, lean the way the bike leans, and let the passenger look over the rider's shoulder on the inside of the turn so the pair move together.

### A first ride together

A short first ride shows whether the pair works before you rely on it:

1. Practise getting on and off at a standstill.
2. Ride a quiet street at low speed, with a few stops and turns each way, braking gently and then a little harder to learn how the bike behaves with both of you aboard.
3. Head for busier roads only after that.


## Day trips two-up

Couples can get plenty from a rental with a day out — Bat Trang, the Perfume Pagoda road, West Lake at sunset — but a passenger tires the rider sooner, so plan shorter distances than you would solo. Stop now and then so both of you can stretch, because a stiff passenger shifts about on the seat and that moves the bike.

Pace the day so you are back on time: the published late-return charge is 20,000 VND per hour. If the plan takes you far from the city, raise it when you book, because support is primarily for Hanoi and local use. ## Questions couples ask

### Does the passenger need a licence?

Article 56(1) puts the licence duty on the person driving, and Article 33 asks the passenger for a fastened helmet and the conduct rules in Article 33(4), not a licence. Whoever takes the handlebars is the person the licence question applies to, which is why the named rider matters.

### Can the passenger hold an umbrella in the rain?

No: Article 33(4) lists using an umbrella among the things a passenger must not do. Carry ponchos for both of you instead, and expect longer braking distances on wet roads. [Renting a Motorbike in Hanoi in the Rainy Season]({{ '/articles/renting-motorbike-hanoi-rainy-season/' | relative_url }}) goes through wet-weather riding.

### Is a Wave or Sirius acceptable for two, or does it have to be a scooter?

The one-passenger limit in Article 33(1) applies to any two-wheel motorbike or moped, so the rule is the same for a semi-automatic underbone. Comfort is what differs, and the two-person bike comparison sets out the trade-offs.

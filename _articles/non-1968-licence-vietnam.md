If your country is not party to the 1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic — or your IDP is the 1949 Geneva format — your home documents do not make you a legal motorbike rider in Vietnam. On the permit itself, Article 57(8) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (No. 36/2024/QH15) recognises only the 1968 format carried with the national licence. Setting aside the treaty routes, the Vietnamese test and the moped class, what remains are two legal routes and one honest conclusion.

In short, a resident whose national licence is still in term can apply to exchange it, whichever country issued it. A rider who also holds a licence from a Convention state can carry that state's 1968 pair. Two treaty clauses in Article 57(6) may reach a licence neither route covers, and only an official source can say whether yours is one. What is left is the Vietnamese licence test, or a bike inside the moped limits, which asks for none.

## Is your country outside the Convention?

The Law lists no countries; it turns on membership of the 1968 Convention, and the record of members is the [United Nations status table][un-status]. ## Route one: exchange as a resident

For riders whose visa or temporary residence card has at least 90 days to run, or who hold a permanent card, [exchange under Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA]({{ '/articles/foreign-licence-exchange-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) is open regardless of convention membership: what matters is a national licence valid in its issuing country, the certified translation, and the residence record. A 1949-convention national licence exchanges exactly like any other valid licence.

Article 24, which took over from Circular 12/2025/TT-BCA on 1 July 2026 (Article 41(6)), is the text to read. Its conditions are three. The applicant lives, works or studies in Vietnam; the national licence is still in term; and the applicant holds a diplomatic or official identity card, a permanent residence card that is still valid, or a visa or temporary residence card valid for at least another 90 days. A person who needs to drive here is "considered" for the matching class, so the office decides, and nothing in Article 24 asks which country issued the licence.

Article 18(7) makes exchange the starting rule. Where a foreigner, or a Vietnamese resident abroad, wants to drive in Vietnam on a national licence, point (a) sends the licence through exchange for the matching Vietnamese class, and point (b) leaves room for a treaty on driving licences that Vietnam has joined. In our reading the 1968 pair of Article 57(6)(b) is the exception point (b) keeps for riders from party states, which leaves a rider from outside the Convention planning around exchange. [The expat licence guide]({{ '/articles/expat-motorbike-licence-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) weighs exchange against the test for a long stay.

## Route two: a 1968-convention permit

Some riders from non-convention countries also hold a national licence from a convention country — long-term residents of a convention country, for example, or dual-licensees. Where the issuing authority legitimately provides a 1968-format permit for your licence, that pairing works in Vietnam like any other.

The pair must still satisfy Article 57(6)(b) and (8). Clause 6(b) asks for an international and a national licence, both suited to the vehicle and issued by a member state. Clause 8(a) describes the permit by its issuer (a competent state authority of a member state), a uniform form, a life of three years at most from issue, and a fit with the national licence's remaining term; clause 8(b) obliges the holder to carry both documents, in term and matching the bike's class. When the licence and the permit come from different states and only one is a party, the text is silent on whether the pair counts, so an official source has to say.

What does not work is a 1949 permit relabelled, or an online "international licence" from a non-governmental seller. The seller's document is not a treaty instrument and has no state authority behind it. A 1949 permit is not the document clauses 6(b) and 8(a) describe, because Article 57 names only the 1968 Convention and clause 6(d) names no treaty. The Law has no sentence refusing a 1949 permit, so the safe step before riding on one is a written confirmation from the traffic police.

## Treaty and agreement routes: clauses (c) and (d)

Clause (c) reaches a licence, suited to the vehicle, of a country or territory that has concluded a treaty or international agreement with Vietnam on recognising each other's licences. Clause (d) reaches a foreign licence, suited to the vehicle, of a state recognised under an international treaty to which Vietnam belongs. Article 30(2) of the Circular says an IDP recognised under a treaty Vietnam has joined is applied as that treaty provides.

Neither Article 57 nor the Circular provisions read for this guide names a country, and this guide names none. The UN table does not decide these clauses either, because it records the 1968 Convention alone: a country with no row may still be covered by one of them. An official source has to confirm, for your country and licence class, whether either applies.

A written enquiry works best when it is specific. Include:

- the state or territory that issued the licence, its class and its expiry date;
- any permit you hold, with its issuing authority and the convention it names;
- the engine capacity or motor rating of the bike you plan to ride;
- the question itself: which part of Article 57(6) covers this combination.

Send it to your own licensing authority or to the traffic police in Vietnam. Ask for the reply in writing and file it with your travel documents.

## The Vietnamese test route

Anyone with no licence, or with one that falls outside the exchange, comes to the test. Article 24(1)(c) rules out a temporary foreign licence, an IDP, a licence past its term, one erased or torn until it no longer carries enough information, one with a difference in identification, and any licence that no competent authority issued.

For classes A1 and A the test is a computer theory paper followed by two riding tests in the yard (Article 4(1)(b) and 4(2)(a)). Beside the training and health certificates, a foreign candidate files a copy of one residence paper from Article 16(2): a visa or temporary residence card with at least 90 days still to run, a permanent card, or a diplomatic or official identity card. [How to get an A1 licence]({{ '/articles/how-to-get-a1-licence-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) sets out the steps, and [taking the test in English]({{ '/articles/vietnam-licence-test-in-english/' | relative_url }}) answers the language question: the Circular makes no provision for an English-language sitting.

A car licence in force waives the theory paper for an A1 or A candidate (Article 4(1)(c)). The text is silent on foreign car licences, so ask the traffic police before counting on it. The paper itself depends on the sitting date: Article 41(6) leaves parts of Circular 12/2025/TT-BCA alive until the end of February 2027, and Article 41(2) brings the new content in from 1 March 2027.

## The moped class

One class of motorbike asks for no licence, so the country on your licence never comes up. Article 34(1)(g) defines a moped by design speed (50 km/h at most) and by motor: a heat engine of up to 50 cm3, or an electric motor of up to 4 kW. Moped riders are left out of the licence duty in Article 56(1), may be 16 or over (Article 59(1)(a)), and must know the traffic rules and be able to control the vehicle (Article 56(4)).

Both tests apply to one machine, and a label decides nothing. A scooter with a 110 cc engine is over the limit, and an electric bike with a small motor still fails if it is designed to exceed 50 km/h. [The moped guide]({{ '/articles/what-is-a-moped-vietnam-law/' | relative_url }}) works through the definition and the checks to make on a particular bike.

## The honest conclusion for tourists

A tourist from a non-1968 country with no residence papers has no legal route to riding a licence-required motorbike in Vietnam on home documents alone, unless an official source confirms clause 6(c) or (d) for the licence. The licence-free categories remain: mopeds and licence-free electrics from age 16, pedal e-bikes under the pedal-bike rules. A licence-free electric is one that meets the moped definition of Article 34(1)(g); an electric bike over either limit is a motorcycle and needs a class.

For anything above the moped line, the answer is a car with a driver, a guided tour, or the exchange after establishing residence. The Vietnamese test and, where an official source confirms one, a treaty route sit beside those three. Exchange and the test both ask for a residence paper from the Circular's list, so measure a short visit against that paper before treating either as a plan. [The tourist overview]({{ '/articles/can-tourists-ride-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) gives the shorter version; the fine amounts it quotes follow the 2024 decree.

Imagine a visitor whose home licence comes from a state with no row in the UN table, booking three weeks in Hanoi with no residence card. The licence does not make the clause 6(b) pair, and nothing in the texts names any country under clause (c) or (d), so the visitor cannot assume either applies. The written enquiry on those clauses can go out before the trip; if none applies, a bike inside the moped limits, a car with a driver or a guided tour are what remain.

## Questions riders ask

### Can a certified translation or an embassy letter make my licence valid for riding?

No. A certified Vietnamese translation is one item in the exchange dossier, certified by a notary office in Vietnam or made by a Vietnamese representative office abroad (Article 24(2)(a)). It supports an exchange; it does not move a home licence into any of the four kinds that Article 57(6) lists as valid in Vietnam.

### Can a car licence from my country carry me on a motorbike?

No. Article 57(1) keeps the car classes apart from the motorbike classes (A1, A, B1), and 57(6)(b) asks for a licence suited to the vehicle, so a licence with no motorbike category carries none. A car licence in force matters only at the test, where it waives the theory paper (Article 4(1)(c)); the text does not say whether a foreign one counts.

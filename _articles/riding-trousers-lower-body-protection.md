Riders armour their heads, sometimes their torsos — and ride in shorts. Yet in a slide, the legs, knees and hips hit first and hardest. Lower-body protection is cheap relative to what it saves, and in Vietnam’s climate the challenge is wearing it without overheating.

## What the armour is for

Two different jobs: abrasion (the fabric between your skin and the asphalt) and impact (armour at the knee, hip and sometimes shin, absorbing the concentrated hit against the road or the bike). In a typical city fall at modest speed, hip and knee points take the load. Standalone knee protection and how to wear it under normal clothes is covered in body-armour-motorbike-riders; this article is about the trousers themselves.

## The options, from casual to dedicated

Armoured riding jeans look like normal jeans but contain abrasion-resistant fibre and hip and knee pockets. For commuters who cannot change clothes, they are the practical compromise. Textile riding trousers with zip vents and removable thermal liners are the all-weather standard — in Vietnam, buy them for the venting, not the liner. Mesh riding trousers are near-transparent to the wind and the honest hot-weather choice. And dedicated off-road knee-and-shin guards are worn over or under trousers for rough riding — the broader off-road kit is in offroad-riding-gear-basics? For city use, vented textile or armoured jeans cover most riders.

## Fit and the Vietnam reality

Armour only works where it sits: knee armour that slides off the knee in the crash is decoration. Trousers should be snug enough that the armour pockets stay located while you walk and sit at cafes, and long enough in the riding position — fabric rides up when you fold onto a motorbike seat. Check armour stays put while actually sitting on a bike, not standing in a shop.

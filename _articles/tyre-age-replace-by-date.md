Tyres wear out twice: once by kilometres, once by years. Riders plan meticulously for the first and have never heard of the second. In Vietnam's heat and sun, the second is aggressive — a tyre can look nearly new and be years past its best.

## Reading the date code

Every tyre carries a DOT date code on the sidewall: four digits, week and year of manufacture — for example a code ending "2423" means week 24 of 2023. It is moulded, not printed, so it is on every tyre ever made. Checking the age of the tyres on a bike takes thirty seconds, and it changes two decisions: buying a used bike (old tyres are common on low-usage bikes — the used-bike checklist is in used-motorbike-inspection-vietnam) and buying "new" tyres (a shop's cheapest stock may be years old; ask for recent date codes).

## Why rubber ages even parked

Tyre compounds contain oils and plasticisers that keep rubber elastic; heat, UV and ozone leach them out slowly regardless of use. An aged tyre hardens: the give that makes rubber grip a wet road disappears while the tread pattern still looks perfect. The failure signature of an aged tyre is not a blowout — it is quiet, gradual loss of wet grip, which is invisible until the day it isn't.

## Vietnam's accelerators

- **Heat.** Hot asphalt and a hot climate speed the chemistry of rubber ageing by design.
- **UV.** A bike parked outdoors in tropical sun ages its tyres while the rider is at work — which is why a bike used weekly but parked outside can have older-acting tyres than its mileage suggests.
- **Flat-spotting and sidewall stress.** Parked long in one position, a tyre takes a set; cracks form at the flex points.

## When age, not tread, is the reason

Consider replacement by age when: the sidewalls show fine cracks, the rubber has gone shiny or glazed, the tyre is past its manufacturer's recommended age (follow the maker's guidance for the specific tyre), or wet grip has clearly faded despite healthy tread depth. The wear-side of replacement — tread, damage, choosing new rubber — is covered in choosing-replacement-tyres-motorbike-vietnam, and the routine care that maximises tyre life is in tyre-care-motorbike-vietnam.

## The three signals in combination

A tyre deserves replacement when any *one* of these is true: tread at the wear limit, visible damage (cuts, bulges, cords), or advanced age with hardening. For low-usage riders — whose tyres usually die of age long before the tread dies — the interplay is explained in maintaining-motorbike-you-rarely-ride.

## The buying note

When a shop quotes you a price, ask the date code before agreeing. A tyre with plenty of tread life but years of shelf age has already spent some of its life in the wrong place — on a shelf. Recent-production tyres at a fair price, mounted and balanced properly (see tyre-valves-wheel-balancing-motorbike), are what you are actually buying; tread depth is only half of what a tyre is.

Valve clearances are the most commonly skipped service item on small motorbikes — invisible, silent when correct, and quietly expensive when ignored. Understanding what they are takes five minutes and saves engines.

## What the clearance is

A four-stroke engine's valves open and close thousands of times a minute, and the metal parts heat and expand as the engine warms. Designers leave a precise gap — the clearance — so the valve still fully closes hot. As parts wear, that gap changes: usually it tightens as the valve seat recedes into the head. The correct figure for your specific model is in the manual and nowhere else — every engine has its own value, which is why this article quotes none.

## Symptoms of valves out of adjustment

Tight valves: hard starting (especially cold), a rough or wandering idle, and eventually a valve that never quite closes — burning its edge, leaking compression, and cascading into real head damage. Loose valves: a distinct, regular tapping or ticking from the top of the engine, loudest at idle, that some riders mistake for a loose component. That top-end tick is a cousin of the knocking family — the sorting guide is engine-knocking-what-it-means, and the idle-drift symptoms overlap with rough-idle-motorbike-troubleshooting. Two-strokes, with their different design, are outside this job entirely — the distinction is in two-stroke-vs-four-stroke-vietnam.

## Why Vietnam's conditions pull clearances off-spec

Constant short trips mean the engine heat-cycles relentlessly; dusty air and stop-start traffic wear valve-train components faster than the manual's optimistic schedule assumes. City-ridden bikes commonly need clearance checks more often than the book's interval — the practical scheduling logic is in motorbike-service-when-vietnam, and it is one of the jobs where "when did anyone last check them?" usually has no answer.

## The job itself

Checking and adjusting clearances means removing a cover, positioning the engine at top-dead-centre on the right stroke, measuring with feeler gauges against the manual's figures, and adjusting where needed. On small Vietnamese engines it is skilled-but-quick work at a competent shop — insist that the mechanic states the figure before measuring, so the number comes from the book rather than experience alone.

Small adjustment, quiet engine, long life. It is the least glamorous half-hour in the service schedule and the one that most decides how many years the engine has.

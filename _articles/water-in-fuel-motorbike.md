Water and petrol do not mix, and that is the whole problem: water sinks to the bottom of the tank, sits where the fuel pickup drinks, and gets swallowed in gulps. Rainy-season Hanoi produces water in tanks by the litre, mostly through habits nobody thinks about.

## How water gets in

- **Condensation.** A half-empty tank breathes: humid air enters, condenses on cool metal every night. Repeated over a wet season, it accumulates. This is why full tanks matter — see the rust-side of the same problem in fuel-tank-rust-prevention-treatment.
- **Flooding.** A bike parked in a flooded street with a poor fuel-cap seal takes water directly.
- **Fuel-station water.** Rare at busy stations, possible at slow rural pumps with old, condensing underground tanks.
- **Washing.** Pressure-washing aimed at the cap or vent lets water past worn seals.

## The symptoms

Water in fuel is intermittent in a distinctive way: the engine runs, then stumbles, then runs, then dies — usually when the tank is low, because that is when the water layer reaches the pickup. A bike that dies with the fuel light not yet on, then restarts after sitting, is describing water. The stumble often coincides with rain or days after flooding. Misdiagnosis as "carburettor trouble" is common because the carburettor is where the water ends up causing havoc — the cleaning procedure is in carburettor-cleaning-tuning-vietnam.

## What water actually damages

Water itself does not burn; a gulp of it kills combustion momentarily and, in quantity, hydraulically stresses the engine. Sitting water corrodes the tank from the bottom, rusts carburettor internals, and attacks fuel lines from inside. Modern petrol containing ethanol can hold small amounts of water in suspension — until the blend "phase separates" and the water-ethanol layer drops out, a layer that both does not burn and corrodes aggressively. The downstream sentries are the fuel filter and tap strainer — check both, see fuel-filters-fuel-lines-motorbike.

## The response

1. Stop refilling over suspicion: fresh petrol on top of water keeps the problem fed.
2. If the bike has a carburettor drain, drain the float bowls into a clear container: water shows as separate beads or a distinct layer under the petrol.
3. If the tank has a drain plug or the tap sits at the lowest point, draining the sump of the tank removes the water layer. Some riders rock the bike and drain repeatedly.
4. Replace the fuel filter after any water event.
5. Water-removing fuel treatments exist and help with small, dispersed amounts; they do not rescue a flooded tank.

## Prevention, honestly

Keep the tank full during the wet months. Check the fuel-cap seal annually and after any fall. After flooding, check the oil too — water that gets past rings turns oil milky — that oil check is part of the wider after-rain inspection in check-motorbike-after-heavy-rain. And use the fuel tap's reserve position knowingly, not as a mystery switch: its logic is explained in fuel-tap-reserve-motorbike.

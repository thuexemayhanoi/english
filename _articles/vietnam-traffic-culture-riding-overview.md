Vietnam is one of the world's great motorcycle cultures — urban researchers count Ho Chi Minh City among the densest two-wheeled cities on earth, and visitors' first video call home is always the same: an intersection where nobody stops and nothing collides. That is not a paradox; it is a system. Vietnamese traffic is not European traffic with the rules ignored. It is a different system with different rules — unwritten, universally known, and learnable. This primer is the theory; the city guides (Hanoi's and Saigon's) are the practice.

## The core principle: flow over priority

Western traffic runs on priority: lights, lanes, and who has the right of way. Vietnamese urban traffic runs on flow: everyone moves at all times, slowly, and the space between vehicles is negotiated continuously second by second. At a "blocked" intersection, bikes do not queue — they infiltrate, advancing into whatever space the cross-traffic leaves, at speeds where everyone can still stop in half a metre. Local operators describe it accurately: it looks reckless and is not; the danger comes from speed and unpredictability, not from the system itself.

**Rider's translation:** your job is to be a predictable, steadily moving object. Sudden stops and swerves are what break the flow — the flow routes around smoothness, and it punishes hesitation mid-intersection.

## The unwritten rules, written down

1. **The horn is turn-signal, mirror and handshake.** A short beep is "I am here" — not anger. Long beeps mean "I am big and cannot stop." Read our horn etiquette guide before flinching your way through day one.
2. **Bigger vehicles have the right of physics.** Buses and lorries cannot negotiate their width; the burden of geometry is on you. Never sit beside a turning bus's rear wheel.
3. **Space is shared, not owned.** Riding two-abreast, filtering between lorries, using the wrong lane to pass — normal, not aggressive. Lanes are suggestions where painted at all.
4. **Speed is the real variable.** The system is safe at city speeds and lethal at highway ones. Locals go slower than they look like they are going; match them.
5. **Lights at night are partial.** Unlit bikes, pedestrians in dark clothing, andbuffalo share rural roads — our night-riding guide is the companion read for a reason.
6. **The u-turn is a national institution.** Vehicles u-turn anywhere plausible; assume the bike ahead of you might, and check your mirror before you commit to a pass.
7. **Rain changes everything for twenty minutes.** Everyone slows, ponchos balloon, visors fog. The first half hour after rain is the highest-risk window — our wet-surfaces guide explains the why.

## How to learn it without donating skin

- **Watch before riding**: ten minutes at a big intersection teaches more than any article. Observe who yields and how the merges resolve.
- **Start small**: a quiet district on a Sunday morning, not a QL highway on a Friday afternoon.
- **Ride at the speed of the locals** — it is a built-in safety governor you do not have to invent.
- **Read the signs guide** for the formal system, then trust behaviour over signage when they disagree (they will).

Vietnamese traffic rewards humility and punishes arrogance. Riders who arrive saying "I have driven for years" and ride like they are at home have the worst first weeks; riders who arrive saying "this is a new language" are usually fluent in three days. The road will teach you either way — but the tuition is cheaper in the second frame of mind.

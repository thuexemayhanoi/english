Hanoi is not a place to learn the absolute basics of riding — the flow starts at every gate. But with a little planning, new riders can build skill safely before joining the river of bikes.

## What makes a good practice spot

- Low, predictable traffic — residential streets, university or industrial areas on weekends, cul-de-sacs and new urban blocks with unfilled buildings.
- Straight sight lines and no blind junctions.
- A smooth surface: sand and gravel teach nothing except falls.
- Somewhere you can be slow without blocking anyone. Busy markets and bus routes are the opposite.

Riverside and dyke roads around the Long Bien area and the newer districts on the city edges are the categories riders typically use — check locally on the day, because weekend events and market days change the traffic of any specific street.

## Timing matters more than location

Early Sunday mornings offer the calmest streets of the week anywhere in the city. The first hour of daylight on any day is quieter than any evening. Avoid rush hours and lunchtime school runs while learning.

## A graduated plan

1. **Hour 1:** empty practice spot — the drills in our first-hour checklist.
2. **Hour 2-4:** quiet residential streets at quiet hours, simple loops with a few turns and one junction.
3. **Next:** a straight arterial street with flow but no merges, at off-peak.
4. **Then:** bridges and the Old Quarter last — the narrow-street guide and the Hanoi bridges guide cover the two genuinely tricky environments.

Ride the loop home the same way each day for a week; familiarity with one route builds confidence faster than exploring ten. Combine with the realistic learning timeline, and resist jumping from step 1 to step 4 in a single afternoon — the riders who struggle are almost always the ones who skipped the boring practice hours.

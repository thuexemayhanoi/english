Rear shock preload is the most useful adjustment most riders never touch — and on Vietnamese motorbikes, loaded with a passenger, a month of shopping or both, it is the difference between a bike that steers and a bike that squats.

## What preload actually does

Preload is the spring's pre-compression — how far the spring is squeezed before you sit on it. Turning the collars (or moving the cam lever on simple shocks) does not make the spring stiffer; it raises the ride height, so the bike sits at the right point of its travel with your actual load aboard. The correct starting figure for your model is in the manual; without it, judge by sag.

## Judging by sag

With the bike on its stand, measure from a fixed point (like the tail) to the rear axle. Sit on the bike in riding gear, feet on pegs, and have a friend hold it upright; measure again. The difference — rider sag — should be roughly a third of the wheel's total travel (the manual or manufacturer's specification states the design value; never copy a random internet number). Too little sag means the shock tops out and the ride is harsh; too much means the bike sags onto its bump stops, steering goes vague and the headlight blinds oncoming traffic. The general inspection habits for worn shocks and forks are in suspension-checks-motorbike-vietnam.

## Passengers and luggage change everything

A pillion roughly doubles the load the rear spring carries. Ride two-up on solo preload and the rear collapses, ground clearance shrinks over Hanoi's speed bumps, and the front goes light exactly when you need it planted — the riding-skill side of carrying a passenger is in balancing-pillion-riding-motorbike-vietnam, and the load-securing side in carrying-luggage-motorbike-safely-vietnam. The maintenance answer is: raise preload for the load, note where you set it, and return it when you ride solo. On shocks without adjustment, the honest answers are softer luggage, better packing or upgraded springs at a workshop.

## When adjustment is not the answer

Preload cannot rescue a worn-out shock: a sagging rear that bottoms out at the solo setting, leaks oil, or knocks over bumps needs replacement, not a cranked collar. And a bike that wanders with correct sag has its problems elsewhere — the wider diagnosis lives in motorbike-handling-problems-wobble.

Two minutes with a tape measure and a spanner makes every loaded ride measurably better.

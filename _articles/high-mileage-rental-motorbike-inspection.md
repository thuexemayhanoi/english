A rental doing real mileage - a commuter's month, a touring fortnight - is a bike consuming consumables faster than a weekend pootle. Long before anything fails, the bike should go back for a look. Nguyen Tu's owner guidance is periodic checks around every 1,500 km of use, which is not a manufacturer interval.

This page covers high mileage rental riding from the bike's side: the check, not the contract. The rental-term side, what to tell the shop when you set up a monthly rental, is in [High Monthly Mileage on a Rental Motorbike in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/high-mileage-monthly-rental-hanoi/' | relative_url }}), and who covers what is in [Maintenance During a Monthly Motorbike Rental in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/maintenance-during-monthly-rental-hanoi/' | relative_url }}).

## The guidance, stated honestly

The owner's practical guidance for high-mileage rental use: bring the bike back for inspection around every 1,500 km of use, or more often if something feels off. This is practical owner guidance - it is not a manufacturer service interval, and it should not be presented as one. Manufacturers publish their own schedules for their own models; a rental shop's guidance is about catching wear on a rental bike.

For the maker's own figure on the model you were given, ask the shop which model and variant it is and read the maker's schedule for that model.

## How soon 1,500 km arrives

Divide 1,500 by your average daily distance, taken from the odometer readings in your log or from a map app. The rows below are plain arithmetic, not typical figures for any rider.

| If you average | 1,500 km arrives after |
|---|---|
| 20 km a day | 75 days |
| 50 km a day | 30 days |
| 150 km a day | 10 days |

Two things follow. A rider who covers a long distance every day can pass the mark inside the first month, so the check belongs inside the rental term, not at its end. A rider on short daily trips may not reach it for a long time, so the weekly habits further down matter more than the odometer.

## What a check covers

A check of this kind looks at engine oil level and condition, chain tension and lubrication (semi-automatic and manual bikes), tyre pressures and wear, brake pads and cables, plus a general look-over of the things daily riders stop noticing. On a commuting bike, 1,500 km can be several weeks of riding; what has worn by then depends on the bike, the load, the roads and the rider.

What the shop itself does is a narrower list. Its maintenance may include an oil change, chain adjustment and tension, tyre-pressure checks and normal inspection. Treat the list above as what to look at, and ask the shop what it will do about each item.

## What high mileage wears first

Mileage multiplies wheel turns, brake applications, chain movement and engine running time. No figure on this page predicts when a given part will wear out. What mileage changes is how often you should look.

### Tyres

The rear tyre drives the bike as well as carrying the load, so inspect it first. Run your eye across the tread for a centre that has flattened or squared off compared with the edges, then look for cracks in the sidewall, cuts, bulges and cords showing through the rubber. If the tyre has wear markers moulded into the tread, check how close the tread is to them; if you cannot find any, ask the shop to judge.

Underinflated tyres wear faster and puncture more, so pressure is the cheap part of the job: read it at a fuel-station gauge. Ask the shop for the pressure for this bike and compare the gauge reading with it. Report a flat centre, cracks, or a tyre that keeps going soft.

### Brakes

A bike that covers many kilometres in stop-start traffic applies its brakes many times, and pads wear a little with each stop. Pad thickness is hard to judge by eye on a bike you do not know, so judge by feel and sound against what you noticed at handover.

Warning signs are a lever or pedal that travels further than before, a squeal or grinding, a pull to one side under braking, or a bike that takes longer to stop. Do not strip anything down yourself. [Brake Squeal and Grinding: What Each Sound Means]({{ '/articles/brake-squeal-grinding-motorbike/' | relative_url }}) explains what the noises can point to.

### Chain

On the semi-automatic and manual bikes, every kilometre moves the chain round the sprockets, so it can stretch and dry out as the reading climbs. [What to Do If the Chain Feels Loose on a Rental Bike]({{ '/articles/loose-chain-on-rental-motorbike/' | relative_url }}) describes what loose feels like and why it should not wait; the weekly look-and-lift below is how you catch it.

### Engine oil

Running time adds up with distance, and oil is the part you can check yourself. Find out at handover whether the bike has a sight glass or a dipstick, then read the level the same way each time, on level ground. [Checking Engine Oil on a Motorbike]({{ '/articles/checking-engine-oil-motorbike/' | relative_url }}) covers the routine.

Write the reading in your log beside the odometer: a level that keeps falling between checks tells the shop something a single glance cannot. Look under the parked bike for drips and report a burning smell. Do not top up with whatever oil is on the shelf; ask the shop which oil the bike takes.

## How it fits a rental

On monthly rentals this is part of the arrangement to settle at the start: the bike lives with you, routine maintenance may be the shop's side of the deal, and the check is a short visit - not an interruption. [Commuting by Motorbike in Hanoi: The Monthly Renter Setup]({{ '/articles/commuting-by-motorbike-monthly-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) covers the commuter's side of a monthly rental. The visit is how the shop's own maintenance items get done, once you have confirmed which of them apply to your rental.

Inner tubes and tyres may be the renter's responsibility depending on the situation, and slow wear from your own mileage is the case where that question comes up on a long rental. Ask who pays for a worn-out tyre before you need one, and agree a replacement with the shop first instead of buying one yourself.

## Between checks, the renter's part

- Watch the oil level (underbones have a sight glass or dipstick; ask at handover which).
- Tyre pressures at any fuel station, weekly-ish.
- Chain look-and-lift weekly (the chain article) on semi-automatic and manual bikes.
- Symptoms reported early, always (the feels-wrong article).

Do the pass in the same order each time - tyres, the chain on semi-automatic and manual bikes, oil, then a feel of both brakes and a glance at the lights - because you spot change faster when the routine does not move.

## An odometer log the shop can use

The odometer turns "I have been riding a lot" into a number the shop can use. The log needs only a phone note.

| When | What to write | What it gives you |
|---|---|---|
| Handover | A photograph of the dashboard with the odometer legible, and the fuel gauge | The reading every later figure is measured from |
| Each pass of your own | Date, reading, what you noticed | When a change began |
| Each check visit | Reading, what was looked at, what was agreed | A simple way to keep count; ask the shop where it counts from |
| Return | The dashboard photographed again | A closing reading to set against the opening one |

The dashboard photograph is part of the [rental motorbike inspection checklist]({{ '/articles/rental-motorbike-inspection-checklist/' | relative_url }}), which also suggests sending the handover photographs to the shop's chat so they carry a timestamp. At the end of the rental, the same photographs and notes go into the return, which [How Returning a Rental Motorbike in Hanoi Works]({{ '/articles/returning-a-rental-motorbike-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) covers.

Log the main odometer, not the trip meter. A trip meter, if the bike has one, can be reset, so it is useful for one journey and useless as a record. If the odometer stops moving or looks wrong, tell the shop, and write down the date and a map-app estimate of the distance since the last good reading, marked as an estimate. Do not try to repair the instrument cluster yourself.

The longer version of this habit, with what else to record, is in [Keeping a Maintenance Log on a Long-Term Motorbike Rental]({{ '/articles/maintenance-log-monthly-rental-motorbike/' | relative_url }}).

## When the bike already shows a high reading

"High mileage" can also mean a rental bike whose odometer was already high when you collected it. A reading is a measure of distance, not of condition: two bikes with the same figure can have been looked after very differently, and a low figure does not prove care either. Do not judge a bike on the number alone.

Photograph the reading at handover as above, run the full handover checklist with extra attention to the four wear items in this guide, and ask when the oil, chain and tyres were last looked at and at what reading. If nobody can say, make those three your first checks and tell the shop what you find.

## What to tell the shop, and when

A report is useful when it carries four things: the odometer reading, the date and reading of the last check, what changed and when it began, and whether it happens cold or hot, moving or stopped. A photograph of the tyre, the chain or the drip under the bike saves a round of questions.

An illustrative message, with your own details in the brackets:

> Rear tyre looks flat across the centre. Odometer [reading]; last check [date] at [reading]. Pressure at the fuel station was [figure] this morning, same as last week. Can I bring the bike in on [day]?

## Planning the check visit

- **Ask what is coming up.** A tyre or other consumable close to replacement is the one to settle before the work.
- **Ask whether the work can be done while you wait** or whether the bike has to stay. No policy on this is published, so ask rather than assume.

## The touring case

A long-distance trip can put 1,500 km on in a week. That is fine - the guidance scales to distance, not calendar. Plan a mid-trip check into any big loop: the shop can advise before you leave on what to watch, and a provincial mechanic can handle the basics en route (keep receipts and tell the shop at return); call the shop first, and report the same day as well.

Before the loop, ask whether the bike may leave Hanoi at all: [Can You Take a Rental Motorbike Outside Hanoi?]({{ '/articles/rental-motorbike-outside-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) covers the question. Support from the shop is primarily for Hanoi and local use, so plan on the nearest help being a roadside mechanic, and ask before any outside workshop touches the bike; [Can You Use Outside Repair Shops on a Rental Motorbike?]({{ '/articles/outside-repair-shops-rental-motorbike/' | relative_url }}) explains how that fits a rental.

## Common questions

### Can I do the oil or chain work myself?

This guide suggests checking; leave the oil change and chain adjustment to the shop. Both are among the items the shop's maintenance may include, and [Maintenance During a Monthly Motorbike Rental in Hanoi]({{ '/articles/maintenance-during-monthly-rental-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) advises against engine work, electrical changes or modifications on a rental bike.

### What if I pass 1,500 km while far from Hanoi?

The guidance scales to distance, so do your own pass at that point, note the reading and what you saw, and tell the shop. For any roadside work, call the shop first, keep receipts and report the same day.

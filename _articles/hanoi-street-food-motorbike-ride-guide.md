Hanoi's food culture and its motorbike culture are the same culture: the city eats on the street, and it gets to the street by bike. Riding to eat — a pho run at dawn, a bun cha street at noon, a crawfish lane at night — is one of the genuine pleasures of having two wheels here, and it works better than any taxi version of the same trip.

## How food streets work

The pattern repeats everywhere: one street or corner becomes associated with one dish — the noodle street, the spring-roll corner, the sticky-rice lane — and a dozen small vendors cluster on the pavement, each with low stools and a specialisation. In the Old Quarter the classic corridors run through the guild streets; every district has its own versions. The busiest hours are after work: from about 18:00, the food streets go from open to packed in ninety minutes.

## The ride

Short and slow. Food streets are, by definition, pedestrian-heavy — the pavement is the restaurant, and the road in front of it is the approach. Ride in at walking-pace-plus, lights on, horn ready for pedestrians facing away, and do not fight for the kerb: someone leaving ahead of you will vacate the best spot within a minute or two.

## Parking while you eat

Three options, in order of localness. **The vendor's kerb**: most street vendors watch the bikes of their eaters — ask by pointing and a smile; this is the normal arrangement and costs nothing beyond buying the food. **The attendant kerbs** on busier streets: a few thousand dong, and the elderly attendant remembers your bike in a way that borders on the supernatural. **The lots**: near the big night-food zones, formal lots exist; use them for longer dinners. The general parking guide covers the conventions.

One rule matters: never park across a vendor's workspace or a shopfront — the food street's economy depends on its frontage, and blocking it makes you the evening's villain.

## Eating and riding

The local sequence is finish-eat-then-ride, not eat-while-riding; the phone-use and riding rules are the safety version of the same advice. In the heat, keep water in the bottle holder for the ride between stops. And build the evening honestly: two or three streets in one night is plenty. Ride between them slowly with the crowd flow — the food streets at 20:00 are the best people-watching in Hanoi from a saddle.

## Where to start

The Old Quarter's food corridors for the classic tourist dishes; the Dong Da and Hai Ba Trung side streets for the versions locals queue for; the night markets for the stroll-and-snack version.

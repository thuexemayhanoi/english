An eight-hour workday is a free full charge waiting to happen. Riders who use it well never think about range; riders who don't, push home on a shrinking display. The gap is mostly etiquette and one question asked once.

## Ask, then charge

Workplace and campus parking belongs to somebody — the building office, the university security desk, the employer. Ask which socket you may use and what the arrangement is. Most answer yes quickly; a socket by the bike park costs them pennies per day. What costs everyone is discovering an unapproved charge running unattended next to the entrance.

## Match the method to the machine

| Machine | Best work routine |
|---|---|
| VinFast-class removable packs | Carry the spare pack up to the office; charge beside the desk; swap at the end of the day |
| VinFast-class with swap access | Skip work charging entirely; swap near home on the route |
| Dat Bike Weaver-class | Charge at work only if the bike parks beside an agreed socket (1 h buys ~100 km claimed); otherwise home-charge overnight |

The removable-pack rider has it simplest: the pack that charges in the office is the pack that never gets stolen off the street, and the two-socket commute — home socket and desk socket — means the machine is always leaving with a full battery.

## The etiquette that keeps the socket available

- One socket, one bike — never daisy-chain chargers.
- Charge during work hours, unplug at the end of the day; nothing stays connected overnight at the office.
- Keep the charger with you, labelled — office chargers vanish like pens.
- Tidy cable runs; a trip hazard turns a favour into a ban.

## When there is no socket at all

Then the swap network is the workplace: V-Green cabinets on the commute route replace the desk socket entirely, and the commuter's guide covers making the whole loop work in Hanoi traffic. Between an office socket and one at home, a mid-range machine barely notices its own range limit.

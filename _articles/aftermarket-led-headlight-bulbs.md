The aftermarket LED conversion is the most tempting bad idea in motorbike lighting: a bright white bulb promises night-riding confidence for the price of lunch. Sometimes it delivers. Often it delivers glare, a broken beam pattern and a bike that sees less than before.

## Why brightness is the wrong measure

A headlight is an optical system: reflector and lens shaped around a filament’s exact position. An LED replacement puts its light-emitting chips somewhere near — but not exactly at — that point, and the carefully engineered beam pattern breaks up: hot spots, dark holes, and above all, stray light thrown above the cutoff line into oncoming riders’ eyes. A bulb that looks brighter in your driveway can see less down the road and blind everyone coming the other way. ## Legal and practical cautions

Lighting requirements for road vehicles exist, and converting a bulb type the housing was not certified for can put you outside them — the required-equipment rules are summarized in motorbike-required-equipment-vietnam, and checking current local rules before converting is your job, not the seller’s. Practically: if the conversion cannot produce a clean cutoff on a garage wall test, it does not belong on the road.

## When LEDs make sense

Purpose-built LED lighting designed as a system — matched to a reflector or a proper projector — can be excellent. On scooters with weak halogen output, a quality LED upgrade with a verified beam pattern genuinely improves night riding, which is a safety topic more than a parts topic (night-riding-motorbike-safety-vietnam).

## The test before you ride

Park facing a wall at night: check for a defined cutoff, symmetric spread, no light above the cutoff. Then ride and check that oncoming drivers do not flash you. If they do, the beam is in their eyes, no matter what the box claimed.

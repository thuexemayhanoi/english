Disc brakes work by clamping a rotating steel disc between two pads, and in Hanoi traffic they do that thousands of times a day. The disc wears so slowly that most riders never look at it — until braking starts to feel wrong. A five-minute inspection catches problems while they are still cheap.

## What a healthy disc looks like

A used disc should show an even, dull grey ring where the pads sweep it, with a slightly raised lip at the outer and inner edges. Fine, concentric scoring you can barely feel with a fingernail is normal wear. Light surface rust after parked rain or overnight humidity is also normal in Vietnam and is wiped off by the first few brake applications — the wider picture of brake checks is in brake-checks-motorbike-vietnam.

## Three findings that matter

**Scoring you can feel.** Deep grooves, a ridge you can catch a fingernail on, or a disc that looks like a vinyl record means grit or a worn-out pad backing has been grinding the surface. Scored discs eat new pads quickly, so replace or re-surface according to what a mechanic advises — replacing pads alone on a deeply scored disc wastes the money; the pad-change procedure itself is covered in brake-pads-replacement-motorbike-vietnam.

**The pulse of warping.** Ride at moderate speed and pull the brake gently: a pulsing, on-off feeling through the lever is the classic signature of a warped or unevenly worn disc. Warping comes from heat (long downhill braking), a hard knock from a fall, or uneven pad deposition. This is not just an annoyance — pulsing reduces control in an emergency stop.

**Rust on the sweeping surface.** Orange patches that do not clean off after riding, or a sticky patch of pad material baked onto the disc, cause grabby, uneven braking. Light cases respond to careful cleaning; heavy cases need a mechanic's judgement.

## Thickness: read the stamp, not the internet

Every disc has a minimum safe thickness. It is either stamped on the disc hub (a number in millimetres, sometimes with "MIN") or listed in the owner's manual. There is no generic "safe thickness" — guessing one risks brake failure. If the stamp is unreadable, the manufacturer specification or a qualified mechanic is the only answer. A set of measurements across several points on the disc tells you whether wear is even; a mechanic can do this with a micrometer in a minute.

## The pairs rule

Replace discs in pairs on the same axle, and pair them with fresh pads — an old disc's ridges chew new pads, and a new disc with oil-contaminated old pads is worse. Never touch the disc surface with greasy fingers: skin oil and brake fluid both reduce friction. If you suspect fluid contamination, the symptoms and responses are in spongy-brakes-brake-fluid-motorbike.

## When to stop riding

Any brake that pulses violently, grabs, squeals metallically with reduced stopping power, or has a visible crack around the bolt holes means park the bike and get it looked at before the next ride.

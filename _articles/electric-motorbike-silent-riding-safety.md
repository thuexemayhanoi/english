The quiet motor is an electric bike's best feature and its biggest hidden risk. In Hanoi traffic, sound is a courtesy petrol bikes give away for free — pedestrians, cyclists and merging cars all track engines by ear. Your silent approach needs to be replaced with something.

## Where silence actually bites

- **Pedestrians stepping into lanes** — especially near markets and school gates, where people walk while listening to nothing at all.
- **Cyclists swerving** without checking, because they heard nothing behind them.
- **Cars merging or turning** from side streets whose drivers scan for what they can hear.
- **Other bikes changing lane position** mid-flow.

## The compensations

1. **Ride where you can be seen.** Position in the driver's mirror of any car alongside; never sit in blind spots. Quiet bikes must be seen, since they are not heard.
2. **Make eye contact before you pass.** At crossings, if the pedestrian has not seen you, slow and pass behind — never thread past a person mid-step.
3. **Use the light.** Modern electrics come with strong lighting (VinFast's Viper, for instance, uses LED projector headlights); run lights even in daytime drizzle and dusk. See our visibility guide for gear and light habits.
4. **Cover the brake in shared space.** Regen makes silent speed changes; get the finger to the brake lever whenever near people.
5. **Use the horn sparingly but genuinely.** A short beep at a pedestrian about to step back is exactly what horns are for.

## Habit of the week

Practise one rule until automatic: whenever you pass anything that could move — a parked car, a market stall, a bus stop — assume it will move, and set your line and speed so that it can. Silence removes the warning; you replace it with positioning.

A scooter tyre can lose meaningful pressure in a week without anything being wrong — temperature swings, permeation, a slow valve. Most riders notice only when the bike starts wallowing in a corner, which is the worst possible way to find out. A TPMS moves that discovery forward to the moment it happens.

## The two kinds of system

**Cap sensors** screw onto the valve stems in place of the dust caps and read pressure in real time, sending it to a small display. They install in minutes, move between bikes, and are the practical choice for commuters. Their weaknesses: they add a little weight to the valve (use metal valves and check the valve for wear — see tyre-valves-wheel-balancing-motorbike for why valve condition matters), and they are exposed to Vietnamese dust and rain, so lifespan is limited by the sensor's sealing, not the battery.

**Internal sensors** mount inside the tyre, like car TPMS. Cleaner, better protected, more accurate readings because they also read tyre temperature — but they need professional fitting and re-balancing, and a tyre change means remounting the sensor.

## What TPMS actually warns you about

The three events that matter: a slow pressure loss over days (leaking valve, porous rim, porosity of an old tyre — when the pressure keeps dropping, the tyre itself may be done: choosing-replacement-tyres-motorbike-vietnam); a rapid loss while riding (a puncture beginning — treat it immediately, as flat-tyre-repair-motorbike-vietnam explains, instead of riding the tyre off the rim); and heat-related pressure rise on long hot runs, which normal systems treat as information, not alarm.

## Vietnam-specific value

Under-inflation combined with long descents, heat and heavy loads is exactly how scooter tyres die early, and pressure checks at a roadside pump are guesswork — the gauges are frequently beaten half to death. A TPMS plus a decent portable pump (portable-tyre-inflators-motorbike) gives you pressure truth anywhere. That said: sensors read their calibration, not reality. Verify against a known-good gauge occasionally, keep valve cores clean, and replace the sensor batteries or the caps when readings drift. TPMS supplements the weekly manual check; it does not retire it.

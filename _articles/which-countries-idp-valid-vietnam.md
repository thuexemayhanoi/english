Vietnam recognises one kind of [International Driving Permit]({{ '/articles/idp-vietnam-motorbike-rules/' | relative_url }}): the one issued under the [1968 Vienna Convention on Road Traffic]({{ '/articles/1968-vienna-convention-idp-vietnam/' | relative_url }}), and only when carried together with the national licence it translates. Article 57(6)(b) recognises that permit; Article 57(8) of the Law on Road Traffic Order and Safety (No. 36/2024/QH15) is the domestic rule that makes the treaty document rideable in Vietnam.

The Law lists no countries. It names the 1968 Convention, and the states that belong to it are recorded in the [United Nations Treaty Collection status table][un-status]: a state with a date in the table's last column is a party, while a state with only a signature, or with no row, is not shown as one.

## The country question answered correctly

The IDP's validity in Vietnam does not depend on the issuing country being Vietnam's neighbour or partner — it depends on the issuing country being a party to the 1968 Vienna Convention and issuing the permit in the 1968 format.

The convention's membership spans a large part of Europe and parts of Asia; Vietnam acceded in 2014, which is what makes foreign 1968 permits acceptable at home, while a 1968-format permit that Vietnam itself issues has no validity inside Vietnam (Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA, Article 30(1)).

The reliable check has two halves: the status table for the issuing state, and the permit document itself, because Article 57(8)(a) describes the permit as one issued in a uniform form by the competent state authority of a member state, so a [permit issued outside the convention]({{ '/articles/non-1968-licence-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) is not the document the Law means. Recognition by agreement between two countries is a different clause, 6(c), taken up below.

## Reading the UN status table for your country

The Law gives no list: Article 57(6)(b) speaks of a state that is a member of the Convention, and nothing in Article 57 names the members. The United Nations publishes the record as the [status table for the Convention][un-status] (Chapter XI-B-19). The copy read for this guide carried the stamp "STATUS AS AT : 27-10-2026".

### Signature, accession, succession and ratification

The participant list has three columns: the state, a signature date, and a last column headed "Accession(a), Succession(d), Ratification". A date there means the state has joined, by accession (a), succession (d) or ratification (no letter). A state that signed but shows no date there has a signature and a blank.

The Law's word is "member" and the table's word is "party". Our reading is that a date in the last column is the table's evidence of membership and a signature alone is not. The page's status line counts 91 parties, and 91 rows carry such a date, which fits that reading.

### Finding your country's row

Search for the name the United Nations uses, which is not always the everyday one: the list has "Republic of Korea", "Russian Federation", "Türkiye", "Netherlands (Kingdom of the)" and "United Kingdom of Great Britain and Northern Ireland". Footnotes can matter. The Netherlands row is marked "For the Kingdom in Europe", and the United Kingdom's notes extend its ratification to Gibraltar, the Bailiwick of Guernsey and the Bailiwick of Jersey (notification of 26 February 2019), saying it otherwise covers the metropolitan United Kingdom only.

Rows are states. If your licence came from a territory or dependency, Article 57(6)(b), which speaks of a state, does not settle it, so ask an official source before riding on that pair.

## Checking your own country, step by step

1. Open the status table and search for your country under its United Nations name.
2. Read the last column: a date means a party; a blank beside a signature, or no row, means the table does not show your country as one.
3. Check which state issued the permit and which issued the licence; clause 6(b) names a member state for both, and the pair is explained below.
4. Confirm on your government's website which body issues permits, since Article 57(8)(a) speaks of the competent state authority.
5. Read the categories and dates on both documents against the bike, using [what a motorbike permit must show]({{ '/articles/idp-vietnam-motorbike-rules/' | relative_url }}) and [how long a permit lasts]({{ '/articles/idp-validity-period-vietnam/' | relative_url }}).
6. If a step leaves you unsure, ask the issuing authority or Vietnam's traffic police in writing and keep the reply.

## What fails

Two categories fail predictably. First, 1949 Geneva Convention IDPs are not the permit that clauses 6(b) and 8(a) of Article 57 describe, because those clauses name only the 1968 Vienna Convention, and the permit has to be carried together with the national licence. A row with a date in the status table shows membership of the 1968 treaty, not the treaty behind a particular booklet, so [how the 1949 and 1968 permits differ]({{ '/articles/1968-vienna-convention-idp-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) is worth reading before you rely on yours.

Second, national licences alone from non-convention countries: no translation, no notarised copy, no rental-shop letter changes a non-recognised licence into a rideable one. Riders carrying these ride in the unlicensed bands if stopped, unless a treaty-based licence under clause 6(c) or (d), covered below, applies. That is shorthand: the amounts sit in a decree amended in 2026, so this guide quotes none; [the no-licence fine guide]({{ '/articles/no-licence-fine-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) gives figures that follow the 2024 decree text, not the Decree 238/2026/NĐ-CP amendment.

## The second half of the rule

Even a valid 1968 IDP is incomplete without its national licence. The rule requires both to be carried together, and an IDP holder who cannot produce the national licence is not carrying a valid licence — the checkpoint treats the permit as a translation whose original is missing. The Law does not call the permit a translation: Article 57(8)(b) says a permit holder riding on a Vietnamese road must have both documents on them, each in term and suited to the class of vehicle.

### Where each document was issued

Clause 6(b) lists the permit and the national licence together and says both are issued by a state that is a member of the Convention. It does not say the two must come from the same state; clause 8(a) ties them only in time, by requiring the permit to fit the licence's remaining validity. For a pair from different states, only one a member, the text is silent, so an official source has to say whether it counts.

## The vehicle-class catch

The IDP translates categories, it does not broaden them. A car-only national licence behind a 1968 IDP gives car categories in Vietnam — riding a 110 cc scooter on it is riding outside category. For rental purposes the check is: does the national licence include a two-wheeled category the IDP actually translates?

Membership does not settle the category either. The Law asks for documents suited to the vehicle, and no text read for this guide converts the category letters on a foreign permit into A1 or A. Article 57(1) puts the A1 limit at an engine of 125 cm3 or an electric motor of 11 kW, with class A above those figures; [what the A1 class covers]({{ '/articles/a1-licence-vietnam-what-you-can-ride/' | relative_url }}) gives the detail, and [the tourist riding guide]({{ '/articles/can-tourists-ride-motorbike-vietnam/' | relative_url }}) is the shorter visitor overview.

## If your country is not a party

A missing date in the last column closes the 6(b) route. Three others are worth checking.

### Treaty and agreement recognition

Clauses (c) and (d) of Article 57(6) add two treaty routes. Clause (c) covers a licence suited to the vehicle from a country or territory that has a treaty or agreement with Vietnam under which each side recognises the other's licences. Clause (d) covers a foreign licence from a state that a treaty binding Vietnam recognises, again suited to the vehicle. Article 30(2) of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA says a permit recognised under a treaty to which Vietnam is party is dealt with under that treaty.

Neither the Law nor the Circular articles read for this guide names a country under (c) or (d), so this guide names none. If you think your country has such an arrangement, an official source has to confirm it, in writing: your own country's licensing authority or the traffic police in Vietnam.

### The moped class

One class of motorbike needs no licence. Article 34(1)(g) sets the moped limits: a design speed of 50 km/h or below, plus an engine of 50 cm3 or below if it burns fuel, or a motor of 4 kW or below if it is electric. Under Article 56(1) the licence requirement does not reach moped riders, and Article 59(1)(a) allows riding one from age 16. Go by the definition rather than the name on the bike: ask what the design speed is and what the engine or motor is rated at. [The moped definition]({{ '/articles/what-is-a-moped-vietnam-law/' | relative_url }}) sets out the text, and [the risks of renting above the moped class]({{ '/articles/renting-a-motorbike-hanoi-without-licence/' | relative_url }}) explain why the line matters.

### Exchange as a resident

Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA makes exchange the starting rule for a foreigner with a national licence who has a need to drive in Vietnam, unless a driving-licence treaty that Vietnam has joined provides otherwise (Article 18(7)). The conditions in Article 24(1)(a) turn on residence, work or study, a licence still in term and a qualifying paper, such as a visa or a temporary residence card that still has 90 days or more to run. They do not mention the 1968 Convention, so exchange does not depend on whether your country is a party, although a permit itself cannot be exchanged (Article 24(1)(c)).

Circular 12/2025/TT-BCA, which older pages still cite for exchange, ended on 1 July 2026 (Article 41(6) of Circular 108/2026/TT-BCA), with a few listed provisions in force to the end of 28 February 2027.

For the dossier and who qualifies, read [the foreign licence exchange guide]({{ '/articles/foreign-licence-exchange-vietnam/' | relative_url }}); for choosing between routes on a long stay, read [the expat licence guide]({{ '/articles/expat-motorbike-licence-vietnam/' | relative_url }}). A rider whose licence cannot be exchanged, or who holds none, is left with the Vietnamese licence test; [taking that test in English]({{ '/articles/vietnam-licence-test-in-english/' | relative_url }}) is a question with its own answer.

## Questions about your own country

### Does Vietnam publish a list of accepted countries?

Not in the texts read for this guide. Article 57(6)(b) turns on whether the issuing state is a member of the 1968 Convention, and the membership record is the United Nations status table. Under clauses (c) and (d), an official source has to confirm whether your country is covered.

### My country signed the Convention but shows no date in the last column. Does my permit count?

On the table as read, a state with a signature and a blank last column is not shown as a party, and clause 6(b) speaks of a state that is a member. The Republic of Korea's row has that layout. Ask an official source in writing before riding on such a permit; the moped, exchange and test routes above do not depend on it.

### Does it matter when my country joined?

The Law names no joining date. Clause 6(b) asks only that the issuing state is a member, and the last-column dates in the six dated rows above run from 9 December 1971 (France) to 1 May 2020 (Thailand) without any difference in how the clause treats them. The dates that matter are the ones printed on your own documents.

### A rental counter says my country's permit is fine. Is that enough?

The counter's answer is its own policy. The legal test is the one in Article 57, and the membership record is public, so look up your country's row yourself. Rental businesses also set their own document checks, which [the Hanoi rental requirements guide]({{ '/articles/motorbike-rental-requirements-hanoi/' | relative_url }}) lists.

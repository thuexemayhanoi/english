Checking oil is the single most useful engine check a rider can do, and it takes one minute. This article covers the technique; it deliberately does not state oil capacities, oil types or change intervals — those are model-specific, and the owner's manual or a qualified mechanic is the only authority for them.

## Where to look

Most scooters and underbones have either a dipstick under a small cap near the engine, or a sight glass on the crankcase — the level should sit between the marked lines, checked with the bike upright on level ground, engine cold or after a few minutes of settling. On a centre-stand bike this is easy; on a scooter you may need to hold it upright. The manual shows exactly where yours is.

## Reading what you see

Level between the marks: fine. Below the lower mark: top up — with the oil grade the manual specifies, not whatever is nearest. Colour is information too: honey to dark brown is normal for used oil; milky pale means water has got in (a humidity-country problem worth a mechanic's attention); glittery or metal-flecked means metal wear — a workshop question, not a top-up question.

## When a top-up is not the answer

An engine that consumes oil steadily, drops dark spots under the bike overnight, or turns its exhaust smoky is telling you something a bottle will not fix. Outside-repair-shops-rental-motorbike covers how small Vietnamese workshops handle such findings, and motorbike-service-when-vietnam covers the service conversation.

## Renters

Oil changes and inspection on a rental are the shop's side of the deal — maintenance-during-monthly-rental-hanoi covers the split. But noticing a leak or a low level early and telling the shop is universally appreciated and protects your own deposit.

## The one-line version

Bike upright, level between the marks, top up with the manual's grade if low — and milky, glittery or disappearing oil is a mechanic's problem, not a bottle's.

Ask a Vietnamese mechanic what a motorbike is worth and the answer comes in two parts: the machine, and the bluecard. The bluecard — the vehicle registration certificate (giấy đăng ký xe) — is the legal ownership document of a Vietnamese motorbike, and touring companies flatly describe it as carrying roughly half the bike's value. Understanding it is non-negotiable if you buy, sell, or plan to cross a border.

## What the bluecard is

It is the state-issued registration certificate that ties a specific frame and engine to a registered owner: registration number, owner name, chassis and engine numbers, and the vehicle's registration details. Rental and touring operators use it as the reference document for exactly what it is — proof of who owns what. The licence plate on the bike corresponds to it; the plate is the public face of the bluecard.

Related documents that are not the bluecard: the circulation/inspection papers for larger vehicles, the compulsory civil-insurance certificate (a separate annual policy — see our insurance articles), and for older machines the original purchase invoice. The bluecard is the one that establishes ownership.

## Why it matters, situation by situation

**Buying.** Check that the bluecard's numbers match the bike, that the seller's name matches the bluecard, and get the ownership-transfer contract signed. Local workshops warn openly that many older used bikes circulate with forged or missing papers — the pre-2013 fleet is the risky zone. If the price is surprisingly low, the papers are the reason. Our buying guide has the full checklist.

**Selling.** No contract + no transfer = you still legally own a bike that a stranger is riding. Do the transfer properly — our selling guide explains the exit.

**Police stops.** A traffic police check that cannot match your bike to its registration becomes a much longer conversation. Carry the bluecard (or a copy, per local practice) when touring.

**Borders.** The Laos and Cambodia crossing guides make the same point: a bike not registered in your name does not cross. The bluecard is the first document a border officer wants.

**Theft and disputes.** If a bike is stolen and recovered, the bluecard settles whose it is. Conversely, buying a stolen bike with clean-looking papers means losing both the bike and the money when the real papers surface.

## Renting vs owning, in one line

Rental bikes come with the shop's papers, which is exactly why rentals cannot be sold, exported or taken across borders by the renter — the bluecard names the shop, not you. That is not a flaw in your rental; it is the system working as designed.

## If papers are lost or damaged

Replacement goes through the registration authority with the owner's ID and the bike itself. For foreigners, the practical advice from touring operators is to keep photographs of every document the day you take ownership — lost-paper problems are solvable at home in a week and painful on a visa.

## The bottom line

Never buy a bike in Vietnam without seeing, verifying and transferring the bluecard; never plan a border crossing on a bike whose papers name someone else; and never ride off on a "cheap" machine whose registration story does not add up. Half the bike is the paper. Buy accordingly.

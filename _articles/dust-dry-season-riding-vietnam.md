The dry months trade Hanoi's rain for dust. On unpaved side roads and construction streets, dust changes four things at once: what you can see, what you can breathe, how much grip the surface offers, and how fast your air filter clogs.

## See and be seen through the cloud

A truck on a dusty road pushes a cloud that hides everything behind it, including you. Never follow through dust at speed — hang back until the air clears, and put your headlight on even in daylight: visibility-riding-motorbike-vietnam explains why lights matter in the murk.

## Breathe deliberately

A good mask makes the difference between arriving with a scratchy throat and not noticing the dust at all. Sunglasses or a visor keep the grit off your eyes — eye-protection-riding-motorbike-vietnam covers options that work in Vietnam's heat. If you wear contact lenses, carry drops; dust days dry eyes fast.

## Grip on the dry stuff

Dust sits like loose powder on unpaved stretches and behaves like fine gravel: steady throttle, no sudden braking, no hard lean. The technique in gravel-roads-motorbike-vietnam transfers directly.

## The bike drinks the dust too

Dry-season riding clogs air filters dramatically faster than you might expect, and a choked filter quietly costs power and fuel economy. Check yours more often in these months — air-filter-care-motorbike-vietnam shows how; the check is a five-minute job.

## Timing and routes

Early morning dust is settled and traffic is thin; midday heat stirs everything up. In the dry season, shift your riding window earlier the same way you dodge the midday heat in hot-weather-riding-motorbike-vietnam — cool air holds its dust down better.

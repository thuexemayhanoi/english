Brake pads are consumables with a courtesy warning period: they squeal, they feel thinner, they take longer to stop you — and then they stop warning. The inspection routine is in brake-checks-motorbike-vietnam; this article covers the replacement side. Pad wear limits and exact parts are model-specific — the manual or a qualified mechanic is the authority.

## The signs that pads are done

Squealing under braking that does not go away after a wet ride, a lever that travels further than it used to, a pulsing or gritty feel, and visible pads worn thin through the caliper. The final sign is a grinding metal-on-metal noise — at that point the disc is being machined by the backing plate, and the repair grows from pads to pads-plus-disc.

## What replacement involves

Pads come in pairs per caliper, and both sides are always replaced together; the front brake usually consumes pads faster than the rear. A workshop removes the caliper, swaps the pads, and — importantly — pumps the lever to seat the new pads before the bike rolls. New pads need a short bedding period: gentle braking at first, not the first emergency stop of their life. Discs are checked at the same time for scoring, warping and minimum condition.

## Cost logic

Pads are inexpensive relative to what they protect; discs and calipers are not. The economics are simple: replace pads at the squeal stage and the repair stays small. Vietnamese workshops do this work quickly and at reasonable cost — motorbike-service-where-vietnam covers choosing where.

## On a rental

Brake work on a rental is the shop's responsibility — report sponginess or squealing and let them handle it. Outside-repair-shops-rental-motorbike covers the process if you are away from the shop when the problem appears.

## The one-line version

Squeal means replace, grinding means you just bought a disc as well — swap pads in pairs, bed them gently, and let the shop handle it on a rental.

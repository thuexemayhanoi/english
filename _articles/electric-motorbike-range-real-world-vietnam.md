Every electric bike in Vietnam quotes a range number, and nearly all of them are honest — under conditions you will not ride in. Understanding the gap between the claim and your commute is the single most useful electric-riding skill.

## What the claims mean

Manufacturers test at steady, low speeds on flat ground. Dat Bike is unusually transparent about this: the Weaver 200 claims 200 km per charge at 35 km/h — but only 130 km at 70 km/h. That 35 percent haircut from speed alone is the honest model for every brand. VinFast quotes 165 km for the two-battery Evo and 156 km for the Viper and Feliz II "under standard conditions" — the same story in different words.

## What eats range in Hanoi

- **Speed.** The biggest factor, as above. Air drag rises steeply; riding at arterial flow rather than district pace can cost you a third of your range.
- **Load.** A passenger and a backpack are battery drain. Expect noticeably fewer kilometres two-up.
- **Stop-start.** City traffic is less punishing for electrics than for petrol (regen recovers some braking energy) but every full-throttle launch costs.
- **Hills and bridges.** Climbing drains; descending with regen gives some back. Hanoi's bridges are short, but long trips into hill country need planning.
- **Ageing batteries.** Capacity fades over years of cycling; a three-year-old pack holds less than its day-one figure.

## Practical planning rules

1. Take the claimed figure, cut it by 25-40 percent for city pace and load.
2. Plan to arrive home with a margin; do not treat 0 percent as a target.
3. Match the model to your real week, not the brochure: a 100 km real-world range covers most Hanoi commutes for days.

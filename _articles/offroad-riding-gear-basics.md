Vietnamese road trips turn to dirt fast: mountain passes, construction detours, the last kilometres to a homestay. Off-pavement riding is a different failure mode — lower speeds, but rocks, ruts, mud and hard ground, and falls that grind rather than slide. The gear shifts accordingly.

## Helmet and eyes

Off-road helmets breathe more and protect less chin-against-pavement — they are built around low speeds and impact, not asphalt abrasion, and they are used with goggles, not visors. For mixed riding (mostly road, some dirt), a vented full-face or adventure-style helmet is the honest choice: an MX helmet on the highway at speed is wind, noise and eye-stream. Choose for the riding you actually do.

## Body coverage

Roost — flying rocks and dirt — is the off-road’s daily grind: a chest protector or pressure vest, and elbow and knee guards, are the core kit. Standalone armour fits over or under clothing; the placement logic is in body-armour-motorbike-riders. Gloves get thicker palms for bar vibration and grip; boots stiffen to protect ankles from rocks and kickstands — riders in light street shoes quickly learn why boots exist. Long, abrasion-resistant layers everywhere: dirt does not slide, it grates.

## The falls are different

Low-speed dirt falls are usually harmless to the bike and rough on wrists, ankles and ribs — gear that guards joints is the point. But never treat off-road confidence as licence to exceed conditions: the riding-technique side of loose surfaces is in gravel-roads-motorbike-vietnam and mountain-pass-riding-motorbike-vietnam, and those articles matter more than any armour.

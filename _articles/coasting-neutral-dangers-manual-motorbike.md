Coasting — pulling the clutch in or knocking into neutral and rolling along with the engine disconnected — feels smooth and free. It is also one of the most common bad habits among new manual riders in Vietnam, especially on the long gentle descents of bridges and overpasses. Here is what the habit costs you, and what to do instead.

## What you lose when you coast

With the clutch in or the gearbox in neutral, the rear wheel no longer turns the engine, so you have no engine braking. Your only slowing tools are the brakes, and on a long descent they heat up. Brakes fade when they get hot: the lever feels the same but the bike slows less. A faded brake on a downhill ramp is a genuinely dangerous situation, and it is entirely avoidable by leaving the clutch out.

Coasting also disconnects your hand and foot from the drivetrain. When you need power suddenly — to escape a car drifting into your lane, or to complete a gap — you must first find a gear and release the clutch. That takes half a second to a full second, sometimes longer with a flustered clutch hand. Half a second at 40 km/h is five and a half metres of dead time.

## The right technique: gear-matched engine braking

Downshift before the descent starts, one gear at a time, so the engine slows the bike with you: releasing the clutch in a lower gear sends a controlled drag through the rear wheel. Choose a gear where the bike holds its speed without throttle — no need to touch the brakes for long stretches. When you do need to slow more, combine gentle brakes with engine braking and downshift again as speed falls. This keeps brake temperature low, keeps the engine connected, and keeps you one twist of throttle away from power.

Two rules make it safe:

- Downshift one gear at a time, releasing the clutch smoothly — never jumping from fourth to second in one click.
- Match revs a little as you release the lever so the rear wheel is not shocked; the downshift guide covers this.

## Where it matters in Vietnam

Long Bien and Nhat Tan bridge approaches, Chuong Duong's downhill ramps, mountain descents around Ba Vi and Thac Dan — anywhere a descent lasts more than a few seconds, engine braking is your main brake and the foot brake is just a trim tool.

## Exceptions, honestly

Coasting a few metres to a stop with the clutch in, in the last metres of a planned stop, is fine. Sitting in neutral at a long red light is fine and saves your clutch hand. The habit to kill is sustained coasting at speed. If you have been doing it, treat every descent for the next week as a deliberate practice session: pick the gear early, let the engine do the work, and stay connected.
